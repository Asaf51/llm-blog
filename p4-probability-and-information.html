Probability & Information: the language of an LLM's output

An LLM never outputs a word. It outputs a probability distribution over 128,256 tokens, and training is the craft of making real text probable. This chapter gives you the probability and information theory the rest of the book leans on: distributions, conditioning, the chain rule, expectation and variance, the √n laws, the bell curve, logarithms, entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence, each worked with numbers.

In this chapter
  • A probability distribution is a table of non-negative numbers that sum to 1. An LLM produces one at every position: 128,256 entries, such as 0.540 for ␣mat in Chapter 4's example.
  • Conditional probability P(A |⁠ B) = P(A, B) / P(B) keeps only the cases where B happened. Chained together, conditionals give the probability of a whole text as a product of next-token probabilities. That product is exactly what an autoregressive language model computes: "The cat sat on" gets about 3.2 × 10−9 from Llama 3.1 8B.
  • Counting: there are C(64, 8) = 4,426,165,368 ways to pick 8 experts out of 64, and the same binomial coefficient gives the unbiased pass@k estimator.
  • Expectation is a probability-weighted average, and variance is the expected squared deviation. Variances of independent terms add, so a sum of d terms grows like √d. A random dot product has a standard deviation of 64 at d = 4,096 and 11.3 at dh = 128, which is why attention divides by √dh. An average of n samples has noise σ/√n: a 500-question benchmark at 70% has a standard error of 2.0 points.
  • The normal distribution puts 68.3%, 95.4% and 99.7% of its mass within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations. Its CDF Φ defines GELU(x) = x·Φ(x). The central limit theorem explains why bell curves appear everywhere.
  • Logarithms turn products into sums. A thousand probabilities of 0.01 multiply to 10−2000, which FP64 rounds to exactly 0 after 162 factors. The sum of their logs is a harmless −4,605 nats. On log-log axes, power laws become straight lines.
  • Surprise is −log2 p bits, and entropy is the average surprise: 1 bit for a fair coin, 16.97 bits for a uniform guess over 128,256 tokens. Cross-entropy is the average surprise under a model. Training an LLM minimizes it, which is the same as maximum likelihood. KL divergence = cross-entropy − entropy. It is never negative and not symmetric, and the direction you minimize decides whether a model covers every mode or locks onto one.
  • Build it: problab, a NumPy toolkit that samples from distributions, checks the √d law and the central limit theorem, multiplies probabilities in log space, measures the entropy of English from a Project Gutenberg book (4.10 bits per character alone, 3.30 with one character of context), computes cross-entropy and KL between texts, and bootstraps the error bar of a 500-question benchmark.

Why an LLM speaks probability

After "The cat sat on the", the next word is probably "mat". It could also be "floor", "bed" or "couch", and occasionally something nobody expected. A function that returns one answer cannot say "probably mat, possibly floor". To capture what language is really like, the output has to say how much weight each option deserves.

That is exactly what an LLM produces. Chapter 1 describes the model as one function f: tokens in, 128,256 probabilities out, one for every entry of the vocabulary. Chapter 4 shows how the last layer turns scores into those probabilities with softmax. Everything around the model then works in the language of probability:

  • Generating. Draw one token from the distribution, append it, repeat. How the draw works, and how temperature and top-p reshape it, is Chapter 14.
  • Scoring. The probability the model gives a whole text is a product of next-token probabilities (the chain rule, below). Its logarithm is the text's compressed size in bits (Chapter 1) and the basis of perplexity (Chapter 8).
  • Training. Adjust the weights so the token that really came next gets more probability. Measured properly, this is the cross-entropy loss, and minimizing it is maximum likelihood (Chapter 4, Chapter 8).
  • Comparing and measuring. How far did fine-tuning move the model (Chapter 9)? How much did 4-bit rounding damage it (Chapter 13)? Is a 1.4-point benchmark gap real or noise? Each question has a precise probabilistic answer.

So the vocabulary of this chapter is the working vocabulary of the whole book. Papers and later chapters use it without stopping to define it: "x ∼ p", "𝔼x∼p", "πθ(y |⁠ x)", "KL(p ‖ q)". Each symbol is introduced here once, in words first, then with numbers. The section Notation decoder and where this shows up at the end collects them in one table.

Systems analogy: a weighted routing table

A load balancer with weighted routing holds a table such as "backend A 50%, B 30%, C 20%". The weights are non-negative and sum to 100%, and each request draws a backend according to them. A next-token distribution is the same kind of table with 128,256 rows. The difference is that the model rebuilds the whole table for every token, from scratch, conditioned on everything written so far.

Distributions: tables that sum to 1

Start with the simplest random thing a programmer can picture: a die. A roll has six possible outcomes. A probability distribution over a finite set of outcomes assigns each outcome a number between 0 and 1, its probability, such that all the numbers add up to exactly 1. For a fair die every face gets 1/6 ≈ 0.167. For a loaded die that favours six, the table might read 0.1 for faces 1 to 5 and 0.5 for face 6, and indeed 5 × 0.1 + 0.5 = 1.

What does 0.5 mean? The practical reading is long-run frequency: roll the loaded die 10,000 times and you will see a six about 5,000 times. (I rolled it 100,000 times in NumPy: 49,683 sixes.) For an LLM the same reading works. P(␣mat) = 0.540 means that if you sampled the next token after "The cat sat on the" many times, about 54% of the samples would be ␣mat.

Notation

A random variable is a quantity whose value is decided by chance, written with a capital letter: X = the face that comes up. P(X = 6) = 0.5 reads "the probability that X equals 6 is 0.5". When the variable is obvious, this shrinks to p(6) or p(x), where lower-case x stands for one particular value. X ∼ p reads "X is distributed according to p", or "X is drawn from p". The Greek capital Σ ("sigma") means "add up" (its sibling Π, "multiply", arrives with the chain rule), and the rule that a distribution sums to 1 is written

Σx p(x) = 1,     p(x) ≥ 0 for every x The sum runs over every possible outcome x: 6 faces for a die, 128,256 tokens for Llama 3. In code: assert (p >= 0).all() and abs(p.sum() - 1) < 1e-6.

An event is a set of outcomes, such as "the roll is even". Its probability is the sum of its outcomes' probabilities: for the loaded die, P(even) = 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.5 = 0.7. Two rules follow directly. The complement rule: P(not A) = 1 − P(A), so P(odd) = 0.3. The addition rule for events that cannot both happen: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).

Two more pieces of notation appear everywhere. maxx p(x) is the largest probability (0.5 for the loaded die). argmaxx p(x) is the outcome that achieves it (face 6). argmax returns the argument, not the value. Greedy decoding is "emit argmax of the next-token distribution", which Chapter 14 shows is a trap for long outputs.

Sampling

To sample from a distribution is to produce an outcome at random with exactly the listed probabilities. Your computer only gives you uniform random numbers: random.random() returns a u anywhere in [0, 1), all values equally likely. Figure P4.2 shows the standard trick. Lay the probabilities end to end on a ruler of length 1, and pick the outcome whose segment contains u. With u = 0.62, the loaded die says six. Chapter 14 owns this algorithm (inverse-CDF sampling) and the Gumbel-max trick GPUs use instead. Here the point is the contract: a sampler is correct if, over many draws, each outcome's frequency converges to its probability.

The next-token distribution is the same object with more rows. Chapter 4's illustrative logits (the raw scores that softmax turns into probabilities) for "The cat sat on the" give ␣mat 0.540, ␣floor 0.243, ␣bed 0.120, ␣couch 0.081 and ␣roof 0.016, which sum to 1.000 because that toy example has only five candidates. A real model spreads a little probability over every one of its 128,256 tokens. Chapter 14 shows why that long tail of individually negligible tokens matters once you raise the temperature.

Joint and conditional probability

The next-token distribution is not one fixed table. After "the" it looks very different from after "a", and after "The cat sat on the" it is different again. We need a way to write "the probability of A, given that we already know B". That is conditional probability, and it is easiest to see on a table of counts.

Take a systems example. A service logs 10,000 requests and records two things about each: did it hit the cache, and was it fast (under 10 ms) or slow? The counts below are illustrative.

Divide every cell by 10,000 and you get the joint distribution: P(miss, slow) = 0.20 is the probability that a request is both a miss and slow. The comma reads "and". The four inner cells sum to 1. The row and column totals are the marginal distributions, P(miss) = 0.25 and P(slow) = 0.23. You get a marginal by summing the joint over the variable you do not care about, which is called marginalizing it out.

Now the question that matters for debugging: if a request missed the cache, how likely is it to be slow? Throw away every request that hit, keep the miss row, and renormalize it so it sums to 1. The result is the conditional probability, written with a vertical bar that reads "given":

P(A | B) = P(A, B)P(B)      P(slow | miss) = 0.200.25 = 0.80 P(A |⁠ B) is the probability of A among the cases where B happened. Dividing by P(B) is the renormalization: the kept row summed to 0.25, and conditioning scales it back up to 1. It is undefined when P(B) = 0.

Two consequences are worth stating as rules. Multiply the conditional formula out and you get the product rule, P(A, B) = P(B) · P(A |⁠ B): the chance of both is the chance of the first times the chance of the second given the first. Write the product rule both ways round and you get Bayes' rule, P(A |⁠ B) = P(B |⁠ A) P(A) / P(B), the tool for flipping a condition. Here P(miss |⁠ slow) = 0.80 × 0.25 / 0.23 = 0.87: most slow requests are misses.

Independence

Two events are independent if knowing one tells you nothing about the other: P(A |⁠ B) = P(A). Equivalently, P(A, B) = P(A) · P(B), so the joint table is just the product of its margins. Two separate dice rolls are independent. Cache misses and slowness are not: P(slow) = 0.23, but P(slow |⁠ miss) = 0.80. Random variables are independent when every pair of their events is. When many draws are independent and all come from the same distribution, they are called i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed), the standing assumption behind most of the formulas in this chapter.

Words in a text are about as far from independent as anything gets. After "The cat sat on the", ␣mat is likely; in a random position of a random text, it is rare. That dependence is precisely what a language model learns. A model that ignored it and multiplied word frequencies together (a unigram model) is the baseline every real model beats. Primer 6 builds exactly the count table above for pairs of words, which is a bigram model.

Common misconception

"A token with 1% probability will never show up." Repeat a 1% chance 500 times, as in a 500-token answer, and the chance that it never happens is 0.99500 = 0.0066. So the chance it happens at least once is 99.3% (complement rule plus independence). Real steps are not independent, but the lesson holds: small per-token probabilities compound over a long output. That is the reason samplers truncate the tail (Chapter 14).

The chain rule of probability

How likely is a whole sentence? You cannot store a table for it. With 128,256 tokens there are 128,2567 ≈ 5.7 × 1035 seven-token texts, and no amount of data would fill such a table. The way out is to apply the product rule over and over, peeling off one token at a time:

P(w1, w2, w3, w4) = P(w1) · P(w2 | w1) · P(w3 | w1, w2) · P(w4 | w1, w2, w3)

P(w1 … wn) = Πi=1n P(wi | w<i) Π ("pi") is Σ's sibling: multiply instead of add. w<i is shorthand for all tokens before position i, w1 … wi−1. For i = 1 the condition is empty (in practice it is the begin-of-text token). No approximation is involved: this is an identity that holds for any distribution over sequences.

Read the right-hand side as a recipe. To get the probability of a text, you only ever need one kind of quantity: the distribution of the next token given everything before it. A machine that can produce that distribution for any prefix can score every possible text and can generate new ones by sampling one factor at a time. That machine is an autoregressive language model. Chapter 1's generation loop samples the factors one by one. Chapter 8's teacher forcing computes all the factors of a training text in one forward pass, and Chapter 5's causal mask guarantees that factor i only sees w<i.

Notice how unequal the factors are. Three of the four tokens are predictable given their context, and one is not. The total is dominated by the surprising token, which is why the per-token view is so useful for debugging models and why logprobs are worth reading.

Optional deep dive: why left to right, and why an end token

The chain rule works in any order: P(a, b) = P(b) · P(a |⁠ b) is just as true. Left to right is a design choice that matches how text is written and read, and it makes generation a simple loop. Some models are trained to fill in the middle by moving a span to the end, which is the chain rule in a different order.

There is one subtlety. The probabilities of all texts of every length must sum to 1, so the model also needs a way to say "the text ends here". That is the end-of-sequence token (Chapter 3): P(text) includes a final factor P(EOS |⁠ the whole text). Without it, the products for all seven-token texts would sum to 1, and so would the products for all eight-token texts, so the numbers would not form one distribution over texts of every length.

Key takeaway

P(w1 … wn) = Π P(wi |⁠ w<i) is exact. An autoregressive LLM is a machine that computes the factors of this product. Generating samples them one at a time, scoring multiplies them, and training raises the factors of real text.

Counting: arrangements and combinations

Two later chapters lean on counting. Chapter 7 sends each token through a few of many "expert" sub-networks, and argues that 64 small experts with 8 active beat 16 big ones with 2 active because there are 4,426,165,368 ways to choose the 8, so each token gets a more precise mix. Chapter 10 scores reasoning models with pass@k, the chance that at least one of k sampled answers is correct, whose estimator is a ratio of binomial coefficients. Three counting rules cover both.

  • Independent choices multiply. A sequence of n tokens from a vocabulary of V has Vn possibilities. For Llama 3 there are 128,2562 = 16,449,601,536 possible token pairs, which is why nobody can count them all (Primer 6 hits this wall).
  • Ordered picks without repeats. Choosing k distinct items out of n in order: n choices for the first, n − 1 for the second, and so on, n · (n − 1) ⋯ (n − k + 1). With the factorial n! = n · (n − 1) ⋯ 2 · 1, that is n! / (n − k)!. For 8 of 64: 64 · 63 ⋯ 57 = 178,462,987,637,760.
  • Unordered picks: divide out the orderings. The router (the part of the layer that picks the experts) does not care in which order it picks its 8 experts, and every set of 8 appears 8! = 40,320 times in the ordered count. Dividing gives the binomial coefficient, read "n choose k".

From counting to probability: the binomial distribution

Run n independent trials that each succeed with probability p: sample 8 answers from a model that solves a problem 30% of the time. The probability of exactly c successes is the number of ways to choose which trials succeed, times the probability of any one such pattern:

P(c successes) = C(n, c) · pc (1 − p)n−c With n = 8 and p = 0.3: P(exactly 3) = 56 × 0.027 × 0.168 = 0.254. P(none) = 0.78 = 0.058, so P(at least one) = 1 − 0.058 = 94.2%, which is Chapter 10's pass@8 for p = 0.3.

In practice nobody knows p. Chapter 10's Build it samples n answers per problem, counts the c correct ones, and estimates pass@k for every k ≤ n from the same samples. Picture choosing k of the n samples at random. The chance that all k come from the n − c wrong ones is C(n − c, k) / C(n, k), so

pass@k = 1 − C(n − c, k)C(n, k)      n = 10, c = 3, k = 4:   1 − 35210 = 0.833 This is the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021). It uses every sample, so it is much less noisy than literally drawing k samples once.
Low-level: binomial coefficients overflow fast

C(64, 8) needs 33 bits and C(256, 8) needs 49, so they fit in an int64. C(1,000, 500) has 300 decimal digits and barely fits under FP64's maximum of 1.8 × 10308; C(2,000, 1,000) has 601 digits and does not fit at all. Python's math.comb uses arbitrary-precision integers and is always exact. In NumPy code, compute ratios as products of small factors instead: Chen et al. write the pass@k ratio as Πi=n−c+1n(1 − k/i), which never forms a huge number. Or work with logarithms (math.lgamma), the general cure described later in this chapter.

Expectation, variance and standard deviation

A distribution is a whole table. Often you want one or two numbers instead: what is typical, and how far from typical things wander. The first is the expected value, the second the variance or its square root, the standard deviation.

The expected value of a random variable is the probability-weighted average of its values. The symbol 𝔼 is read "the expected value of":

𝔼[X] = Σx p(x) · x        𝔼x∼p[f(x)] = Σx p(x) · f(x) The second form averages any function f of the outcome. The subscript x ∼ p names the distribution the average is taken over; it matters as soon as two distributions are in play, as in cross-entropy and KL later.

For a fair die, 𝔼[X] = (1 + 2 + ⋯ + 6)/6 = 3.5, a value the die can never show. The expected value is a centre of mass, not a likely outcome. For the loaded die it is 0.1 × (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5) + 0.5 × 6 = 4.5.

The single most useful property is linearity: 𝔼[aX + b] = a 𝔼[X] + b, and 𝔼[X + Y] = 𝔼[X] + 𝔼[Y] always, even when X and Y are dependent. Two loaded dice sum to 9 on average. The expected latency of a request pipeline is the sum of the expected stage latencies, however correlated the stages are.

The variance measures spread: the expected squared distance from the mean μ ("mu") = 𝔼[X]. The standard deviation σ (lower-case "sigma") is its square root, which brings the units back to those of X:

Var[X] = 𝔼[(X − μ)2] = 𝔼[X2] − μ2        σ = √Var[X] Fair die: Var = 15.17 − 12.25 = 2.92, σ = 1.71. Loaded die: Var = 23.5 − 20.25 = 3.25, σ = 1.80. The square in the definition makes big deviations count much more than small ones.

Scaling a variable scales its standard deviation: Var[aX + b] = a2 Var[X], so σ grows by |a| and the shift b does nothing. That gives the most common transformation in deep learning, standardizing: z = (x − μ)/σ has mean 0 and standard deviation 1, and z says "how many standard deviations above average". Chapter 4's LayerNorm standardizes the 4,096 numbers of each token. Chapter 10's GRPO standardizes the rewards of a group of rollouts (several sampled answers to the same prompt): rewards [1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0] have μ = 0.375 and σ = 0.484, so the winners get (1 − 0.375)/0.484 = +1.29 and the losers −0.77.

Estimating an expectation from samples

You almost never know p exactly. What you have are samples x1, …, xn drawn from it. The sample mean x̄ = (1/n) Σi xi estimates 𝔼[X], and the same works for any function:

𝔼x∼p[f(x)] ≈ 1n Σi=1n f(xi),     xi ∼ p The ≈ ("approximately equal") gets better as n grows; the next section says exactly how fast. Estimating an expectation by averaging random samples is called a Monte Carlo estimate.

This one line is how every 𝔼 in the book is evaluated in practice. The training loss of Chapter 8 is an average over the tokens of a batch. The KL penalty of Chapter 9's RLHF objective (a measure of how far the model has drifted, defined at the end of this chapter) is estimated from answers the model itself sampled. The 𝔼 at the front of GRPO's objective is an average over prompts and rollouts.

Gotcha: n or n − 1?

Dividing the squared deviations of a sample by n slightly underestimates the true variance, because the sample mean sits closer to the sample than the true mean does. Dividing by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) fixes the bias. NumPy's np.std divides by n by default (ddof=0); PyTorch's torch.std divides by n − 1 by default (correction=1). For thousands of samples the difference is invisible. For GRPO's group of 8 it is not: 0.484 versus 0.518. Chapter 10's figure uses the first.

Sums grow like √n, averages shrink like 1/√n

A dot product of two 4,096-dimensional vectors is a sum of 4,096 products. Suppose each product is a random number of typical size 1, as likely positive as negative. How big is the sum? Not 4,096. The positive and negative terms mostly cancel, and the typical size is √4,096 = 64. This one fact explains a scale factor in every transformer layer, the size of weights at initialization, and why most leaderboard gaps are noise.

Variances add

For independent random variables, variances add:

Var[X + Y] = Var[X] + Var[Y]     ⇒     Var[X1 + ⋯ + Xn] = nσ2,    std = σ√n The second form is for n i.i.d. terms that each have standard deviation σ. Standard deviations do not add; their squares do. (Why: expanding (X + Y − μX − μY)2 gives the two variances plus a cross term 2 𝔼[(X − μX)(Y − μY)], the covariance, which is 0 for independent variables.)

Apply it to a dot product q · k = Σi qiki, where all the entries are independent with mean 0 and variance 1. Each product qiki has mean 0 and variance 𝔼[qi2] 𝔼[ki2] = 1. Adding d of them gives variance d, so the dot product's standard deviation is √d. That is 64 at Llama's model width d = 4,096, and 11.3 at its head size dh = 128. Over 20,000 random pairs I measured 64.2 and 11.31. (The cosine of two random directions is this dot product divided by the two lengths, about √d each, which is why Chapter 4 finds cosines of size 1/√d.)

Why does the size matter? Attention feeds such dot products into softmax (Chapter 5). Scores that differ by 10 or 20 make softmax put essentially all weight on one key, and a saturated softmax has almost no gradient, so training stalls. Across 4,000 random trials with 64 keys, the largest raw softmax weight averaged 0.85, and in a third of the trials it exceeded 0.99. After dividing the scores by √128 the largest weight averaged 0.11. Dividing by √dh is exactly the correction that undoes the √d growth; Chapter 5's Why divide by √dh? has the rest of the argument.

The same arithmetic sets the scale of freshly initialized weights. An output of a linear layer y = Wx is a sum of n products wijxj, so Var[y] = n · Var[w] · Var[x]. To keep signals at the same size layer after layer, the weights need variance 1/n, a standard deviation of 1/√n. For n = 4,096 inputs that is 1/64 ≈ 0.0156; with standard-normal weights, the output's standard deviation would be 64 times its input's. (Llama 3.1's config.json sets initializer_range to 0.02, the same ballpark. Chapter 4's Build it tries He initialization, √(2/n), which compensates for ReLU zeroing half its inputs.)

Averages: the law of large numbers and σ/√n

Divide a sum of n i.i.d. terms by n and you get their average. Dividing by n divides the variance by n2, so the average has variance nσ2/n2 = σ2/n:

std of the average of n samples = σ√n    (the standard error) The average of 100 rolls of the loaded die has mean 4.5 and standard error 1.80/√100 = 0.18. To halve the noise of any average you need four times as many samples.

The standard error shrinks to 0 as n grows, so the average converges to the expected value. That is the law of large numbers, the reason the Monte Carlo estimate of the previous section works at all. The 1/√n rate tells you what it costs.

A benchmark score is such an average. Each question is a 0/1 variable that equals 1 with probability p (the model's true accuracy on questions like these). A 0/1 variable has mean p and variance p(1 − p). So the accuracy measured on n questions has standard error √(p(1 − p)/n). For n = 500 questions at p = 0.70, that is √(0.21/500) = 0.0205, or 2.0 percentage points. An interval of ±1.96 standard errors, here ±4.0 points, catches the true accuracy 95% of the time (the 1.96 comes from the bell curve of the next section). A model that truly scores 70% can easily post 66% or 74%. In one simulated run of exactly that, I got 66.2%.

Comparing two models doubles the trouble. If they answer different random questions, the variance of the difference is the sum of the two variances, so the standard error of the gap is about √2 × 2.0 = 2.9 points. I simulated 20,000 pairs of 500-question runs for a model with true accuracy 70% and one with 72%: the worse model scored at least as high as the better one in 25% of them. Scoring both on the same questions helps, because the questions that are hard for one tend to be hard for the other, and a paired comparison cancels that shared part (Miller, 2024, in Further reading). Sampling at temperature above 0 adds a second source of noise per question, which averaging several samples per question reduces. Chapter 9 covers how evaluations are run; this section is why their small differences need error bars.

Common misconception

"Model A scored 71.2% and model B 69.8% on a 500-question benchmark, so A is better." The 1.4-point gap is half the 2.9-point standard error of the difference. It is what pure noise produces all the time. On AIME 2024's 30 problems the standard error at 70% is 8.4 points; on MMLU's 14,042 questions it is 0.39. Before you read a gap, compute √(p(1 − p)/n).

The same law governs training. A minibatch gradient is an average of per-example gradients, so its noise falls as 1/√(batch size). Primer 1 introduces minibatches as estimates, and Chapter 8 covers how batch size and learning rate interact.

Continuous distributions and the bell curve

Weights, activations and noise are real numbers, not faces of a die. A real-valued random variable can take infinitely many values, so each single value has probability 0: P(X = 0.5 exactly) = 0. Instead of a table, a continuous distribution has a density p(x), a curve whose area gives probability:

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab p(x) dx,     ∫−∞∞ p(x) dx = 1 ∫ (the integral sign) reads "the area under the curve, from a to b". It is the continuous version of Σ: chop the range into thin slices of width dx and add up p(x) · dx. Expectations work the same way, with ∫ in place of Σ: 𝔼[X] = ∫ x p(x) dx.

A density is not a probability and can exceed 1. Uniform on [0, 0.5] has density 2, because its area must still be 1. The uniform distribution U(0, 1) has density 1 on [0, 1] and 0 elsewhere. It is what random.random() returns, with mean 0.5, variance 1/12 = 0.083 and standard deviation 0.289.

The normal distribution

The normal or Gaussian distribution N(μ, σ2) is the bell curve, centred at μ with standard deviation σ:

p(x) = 1σ√(2π) exp(−(x − μ)22σ2) Careful: the second argument of N(μ, σ2) is the variance, so N(0, 0.022) has standard deviation 0.02. N(0, 1) is the standard normal, which np.random.standard_normal and torch.randn draw from. Its peak density is 1/√(2π) = 0.399.

Three numbers are worth memorizing, the 68–95–99.7 rule: a normal variable lands within 1σ of its mean 68.3% of the time, within 2σ 95.4%, and within 3σ 99.7%. A value 6σ out happens about twice in a billion draws. So a reading of "5σ from the mean" is a strong hint that something other than normal noise is going on.

The running total of a distribution is its cumulative distribution function (CDF), F(x) = P(X ≤ x). The ruler in Figure P4.2 was the loaded die's CDF laid flat; Chapter 14 samples tokens by inverting it. The standard normal's CDF has its own symbol, Φ ("phi"). Φ(0) = 0.5 by symmetry, Φ(1) = 0.841 and Φ(1.96) = 0.975, which is where the 1.96 of a 95% interval comes from: 2.5% of the area lies beyond each end. Chapter 4's GELU activation is GELU(x) = x · Φ(x), so GELU(1) = 0.841 and GELU(−1) = −1 × 0.159 = −0.159.

The central limit theorem

Why is the bell curve everywhere? The central limit theorem (CLT) says that the sum, or the average, of many independent terms is approximately normal, almost whatever the distribution of each term. The terms only need a finite variance. The mean and the standard deviation come from the previous section (μ and σ/√n for an average); the CLT adds the shape. A single loaded die is lopsided, an average of 12 of them is already close to a bell, and the classic trick of adding 12 uniform numbers and subtracting 6 gives a passable N(0, 1). Dot products of random vectors, benchmark scores and gradient noise are all sums of many small pieces, so they come out bell-shaped. Try it below.

Caveat: real tails are fatter than the bell

The CLT describes the middle of a distribution well and its far tails poorly, and it needs independent terms with finite variance. Real LLM activations break the bell shape badly: a few channels carry values dozens of standard deviations from the mean, far more often than a normal distribution allows. Those outliers are the central problem of activation quantization (Chapter 13). Assume normality for back-of-the-envelope estimates, and check the tails before you rely on it.

Logarithms: products into sums, power laws into lines

The chain rule multiplies thousands of probabilities, most of them small. Scaling-law plots span ten orders of magnitude. Both problems have the same cure, the logarithm, and it is worth relearning the handful of rules properly.

exp and log

The number e ≈ 2.71828 is the base of the natural exponential, exp(x) = ex. It is always positive, exp(0) = 1, and it turns sums into products: ea+b = ea · eb. It also grows explosively, which is why softmax needs care (Chapter 4). The logarithm undoes an exponential: logb x is the power you must raise b to in order to get x. So log2 8 = 3, log10 1,000 = 3, and the natural log ln x = loge x. In ML papers a bare "log" almost always means ln; in information theory it often means log2. Context, or the unit (nats or bits), tells you which.

Why we work with log-probabilities

Score a 1,000-token text whose tokens each got probability around 0.01. The chain rule says P(text) = 0.011000 = 10−2000. No floating-point format can hold that. The smallest positive FP64 number is a subnormal of about 4.9 × 10−324 (the smallest normal one is 2.2 × 10−308), and FP32's is 1.4 × 10−45 (Chapter 2 explains subnormals). Multiply 0.01 into a running product and the result becomes exactly 0.0 after 162 factors in FP64 and after 23 in FP32. After that, every text scores 0 and no two can be compared.

The fix is to never form the product. Store and add log-probabilities instead: log P(text) = Σi log P(wi |⁠ w<i). The same 1,000 tokens give 1,000 × ln 0.01 = −4,605.2 nats, an ordinary number. This is why APIs return logprobs rather than probabilities, why the training loss is a sum of logs, and why Figure P4.4 ended in bits.

Sometimes you need to add probabilities you hold as logs, for example to get P(A or B) from log P(A) = −1,000 and log P(B) = −1,001. Exponentiating first gives exp(−1,000) = 0 in FP64, and log 0 = −∞. The log-sum-exp trick factors out the largest term m first:

log(ea + eb) = m + log(ea−m + eb−m),    m = max(a, b) With a = −1,000 and b = −1,001: −1,000 + ln(1 + e−1) = −1,000 + 0.3133 = −999.687. Every exponent is now ≤ 0, so nothing overflows, and the largest term is exactly 1, so nothing underflows to a zero sum. NumPy has it as np.logaddexp.

This is the same move as Chapter 4's max-subtraction in softmax, and Chapter 4 owns the details (log-softmax, and why the LM loss is computed straight from logits).

Power laws and log-log plots

A power law is a relationship y = a · xk: multiplying x by a constant factor multiplies y by a constant factor, wherever you start. Take logs of both sides and the rules above give

log y = log a + k · log x A straight line in the variables log x and log y, with slope k. Plot a power law on axes whose tick marks go 1, 10, 100, 1,000 (log-log axes) and it is a straight line. An exponential y = a ekx is a straight line on a different plot: log y against plain x (semi-log axes).

Scaling laws are power laws. Chapter 8 quotes the Chinchilla law (DeepMind, 2022: loss as a function of model size and training data) as refit by Epoch AI in 2024, whose model-size term is 482/N0.348. Every 10× more parameters multiplies that term by 10−0.348 = 0.449: it falls from 0.793 at 100 million parameters to 0.160 at 10 billion and 0.032 at a trillion. On linear axes the curve is a useless L-shape; on log-log axes it is a line you can extend with a ruler, which is exactly how labs forecast a giant run from small ones. The same chapter's older Kaplan law (OpenAI, 2020) L(N) = (Nc/N)0.076 has a much shallower slope: 10× more parameters cuts the loss by only 16% (10−0.076 = 0.839).

Systems analogy: latency percentiles and log axes

You already read log axes: latency histograms with buckets at 1, 10, 100 and 1,000 ms, or a roofline plot (Chapter 2), whose sloped and flat segments are power laws with exponents 1 and 0. The rule for reading any log-log plot: equal distances mean equal ratios, and a slope of −0.35 means "10× more input, 0.45× the output".

Surprise and entropy

How much information is there in learning the next token? If the model was sure, you learned nothing. If it gave the token 1%, you learned a lot. Information theory turns this intuition into a number, and the number turns out to be the compressed size of the outcome.

The surprise (or self-information) of an outcome that had probability p is −log2 p, measured in bits. A certain outcome (p = 1) costs 0 bits. Each halving of the probability adds exactly 1 bit: 0.5 costs 1 bit, 0.25 costs 2, 1/1,024 costs 10. A 1% event costs 6.64 bits. Measured with the natural log instead, −ln p, the unit is the nat (1 nat = 1.443 bits).

Why a logarithm? Two reasons, one practical and one structural. Practically, picking one item out of 2k equally likely ones takes k yes/no questions, or a k-bit index: a hash table with 1,024 buckets needs a 10-bit bucket number. Structurally, the surprises of independent events should add, and since their probabilities multiply, only a logarithm turns one into the other. Chapter 1's Prediction is compression uses this directly: an arithmetic coder driven by the model stores each token in about −log2 p bits, which is why ␣cat costs 13.7 bits in Figure 1.9.

Cross-entropy and maximum likelihood

A model never knows the true distribution of text. It has its own distribution q, and the world draws from some true p. If you encode outcomes from p with a code built for q, or equivalently, if you are surprised according to q while the world behaves according to p, what do you pay on average? The answer is the cross-entropy:

H(p, q) = 𝔼x∼p[−log q(x)] = − Σx p(x) log q(x) The outcomes come from p (the subscript of 𝔼), and the surprise is measured with q. With log2 the unit is bits; with ln it is nats. The order matters: H(p, q) and H(q, p) are different numbers.

Worked example. The world rolls the loaded die (p), but your model believes the die is fair (q = 1/6 everywhere). Every outcome then costs log2 6 bits, so H(p, q) = 2.585 bits, against the 2.161 bits you would pay with the right model. Reverse the roles, a fair world and a model that believes in the loading, and you pay H(fair, loaded) = 2.935 bits instead of 2.585. In both cases the wrong model costs extra. That is a theorem (Gibbs' inequality): H(p, q) ≥ H(p), with equality only when q = p. The best possible model of a source is the source itself.

The loss of a language model is a cross-entropy

You cannot evaluate Σx p(x) … when p is "all English text". But you have samples from p: real text. The Monte Carlo rule turns the expectation into an average over them:

H(p, qθ) ≈ 1n Σi=1n −log qθ(xi),     xi ∼ p θ ("theta") stands for all the model's parameters; qθ is the distribution those parameters define. Each term is the model's surprise at a real outcome, its negative log-likelihood. For a language model, xi is the actual next token and qθ is the next-token distribution given the real prefix.

That average is exactly the training loss of Chapter 4 (one position: −ln pcorrect, 0.616 nats for ␣mat) and of Chapter 8 (averaged over every position of a batch). PyTorch's F.cross_entropy computes it in nats. Perplexity is the same number in friendlier units, eloss in nats = 2loss in bits, which Chapter 8 owns.

Maximum likelihood is the same thing

The likelihood of a model is the probability it assigns to the data you actually observed, Πi qθ(xi). Maximum likelihood chooses the parameters that make it largest. Taking the log (which keeps the same winner, and avoids underflow) turns the product into a sum. Dividing by −n turns "maximize" into "minimize" and the sum into the average above. So three phrases describe one procedure:

  • maximize the likelihood of the training data,
  • minimize the cross-entropy between the data and the model (the average negative log-likelihood),
  • minimize the compressed size of the data under the model's code (Chapter 1).

A tiny worked example shows what the optimum looks like. You flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads. A model is one number, q = P(heads). Its average negative log-likelihood on your data is −(0.7 log2 q + 0.3 log2(1 − q)):

The minimum sits at q = 0.7, the observed frequency, where the cross-entropy equals the entropy of the data itself, H(0.7) = 0.881 bits. In general, the maximum-likelihood estimate of a categorical distribution is just its counted frequencies, which is how the n-gram models of Primer 6 are fitted. A neural network cannot be solved by counting, so it is fitted by gradient descent on the same loss (Primer 3, Chapter 8).

Two practical notes. First, the loss on the training data can be pushed below what generalizes, by memorizing; the cross-entropy that matters is measured on held-out text (Primer 1 covers overfitting). Second, notation for models varies. Pretraining papers write pθ(x), and RL papers write πθ(y |⁠ x), read "the policy's probability of answer y given prompt x" (policy is reinforcement learning's word for the model that chooses actions, here tokens) (Chapters 9 and 10). Both mean "the distribution defined by the network with weights θ".

Intuition: why the log, and not just the probability?

Why not maximize the average probability of the data instead? Because the log punishes a near-zero on any single example without limit: if the model gives a real token probability 10−9, that token alone costs 30 bits. So a model trained with cross-entropy learns never to rule anything out that actually occurs. It spreads probability over every plausible continuation rather than betting on one. The next section names this behaviour: it is the "mode-covering" direction of KL divergence.

KL divergence: the price of the wrong distribution

Cross-entropy mixes two things: the irreducible uncertainty of the data, H(p), and the extra cost of using q instead of p. The extra cost alone is the Kullback–Leibler divergence:

KL(p ‖ q) = H(p, q) − H(p) = Σx p(x) log p(x)q(x) = 𝔼x∼p[log p(x) − log q(x)] Read KL(p ‖ q) as "the KL divergence of q from p", or simply "KL of p against q". The double bar separates the two arguments, and the first one is the distribution the average is taken over. Also written DKL(p ‖ q).

Its properties follow from the cross-entropy ones.

  • Never negative, and 0 only for identical distributions. That is Gibbs' inequality restated. It makes KL a natural "how different are these?" score.
  • Not symmetric. KL(loaded ‖ fair) = 2.585 − 2.161 = 0.424 bits, but KL(fair ‖ loaded) = 2.935 − 2.585 = 0.350 bits. Because of this, and because it fails the triangle inequality, KL is called a divergence rather than a distance.
  • Infinite where q rules out what p allows. If q(x) = 0 but p(x) > 0, the term log(p/0) is infinite. The opposite case, p(x) = 0, contributes nothing.
  • Minimizing cross-entropy minimizes KL. H(p) is fixed by the data, so pretraining, which lowers H(pdata, qθ), is equivalently pushing KL(pdata ‖ qθ) toward 0.

Forward and reverse KL

The asymmetry has a visible consequence when q is too simple to match p. Suppose p has two humps (two equally good answers, say) and q can only be a single bell. Which bell is "closest" depends on which KL you minimize (Figure P4.12):

  • Forward KL, KL(p ‖ q), averages over p. Wherever p has mass and q is small, log(p/q) is huge. So q must spread out and cover every hump, even at the price of putting mass in the valley between them. This is called mode-covering.
  • Reverse KL, KL(q ‖ p), averages over q. Now the penalty is for putting q's mass where p is small. A wide bell is punished for its mass in the valley, so the best q sits on one hump and ignores the other. This is called mode-seeking.

The same thing happens with six discrete outcomes. Let p = [0.45, 0.04, 0.01, 0.01, 0.04, 0.45], with two modes at the ends. Compare a wide q = [0.20, 0.15, 0.15, 0.15, 0.15, 0.20] with a narrow q = [0.88, 0.07, 0.02, 0.01, 0.01, 0.01]. Forward KL prefers the wide one (0.82 bits against 2.07). Reverse KL prefers the narrow one (0.85 bits against 1.28). Both presets are in the widget below: drag the bars and watch the two KL values.

Optional deep dive: GRPO's strange KL formula

Chapter 10 estimates the per-token KL with r − ln r − 1, where r = πref/πθ for the token the policy sampled. Why not just ln(πθ/πref) = −ln r, the term inside KL's expectation? Both are valid Monte Carlo estimates of KL(πθ ‖ πref), because the extra part r − 1 has expectation 0 under the policy: 𝔼y∼πθ[πref(y)/πθ(y)] = Σy πref(y) = 1. But −ln r alone is negative whenever the reference likes a token more than the policy does, which makes single-token estimates noisy and sometimes negative. r − ln r − 1 is never negative (the curve r − 1 lies above ln r everywhere), so every token's estimate is ≥ 0. It is still unbiased, and in John Schulman's experiments (Approximating KL Divergence, where it is called k3) its standard deviation is many times smaller when the two distributions are close. Chapter 10's token with πref = 0.20 and πθ = 0.23 gives r = 0.870 and 0.870 − ln 0.870 − 1 = 0.0093.

Key takeaway

KL(p ‖ q) = cross-entropy − entropy: the extra bits for using q when the world is p. It is ≥ 0, it is 0 only when the two are identical, and it is not symmetric. Averaging over p (forward) makes q cover everything p does. Averaging over q (reverse) makes q avoid everything p considers unlikely.

The mental model to carry forward

An LLM is a conditional distribution P(next token |⁠ context), and the chain rule turns it into a distribution over whole texts. Everything is computed in logs. Training minimizes the average surprise of real text, which is cross-entropy, which is maximum likelihood, which is compression. Distributions are compared with KL, whose direction matters. Every measured number, from a dot product to a benchmark score, carries noise that shrinks only as 1/√n.

Hints (open only when stuck)
  • Entropy is nan: 0 × log 0 evaluates to 0 × (−∞) = nan. Drop zero entries first: p = p[p > 0].
  • KL comes out negative: the two arrays are not both normalized, or they are over different alphabets or in a different order. Assert both sum to 1 and have the same length.
  • The FP32 product never reaches 0: Python floats are FP64, and np.float32(x) * 0.01 can upcast. Keep both factors in np.float32.
  • Sampler off by one: np.searchsorted(cdf, u, side="right") returns the segment index directly. Compare your frequencies with rng.choice(len(p), size=n, p=p).
  • Bigram entropy far below 3 bits: you probably divided the pair counts by the grand total and took the entropy of that joint table (about 7.40 bits) minus the wrong marginal. H(previous, next) − H(previous) ≈ 7.40 − 4.10 = 3.30.
  • Your unigram entropy is about 4.5, not 4.1: you skipped normalization and counted raw characters with case and punctuation (I got 4.53 bits over 96 distinct byte values). Both are valid measurements; they are different alphabets.
  • You get 4.100 and 3.312, not 4.098 and 3.305: you kept Project Gutenberg's English header and licence text. Cut at the marker lines first; the stripped book has 692,488 normalized symbols.
  • Gutenberg refuses the download: it throttles repeated requests. Cache each file in data/ after the first success and never download in a loop.

Further reading

  • Claude E. Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication (1948). The paper that defined entropy and proved the source coding theorem. The first sections are readable with this chapter in hand.
  • Claude E. Shannon, Prediction and Entropy of Printed English (1951). Shannon measures the entropy of English with n-gram statistics and a human guessing game, an early ancestor of language-model evaluation.
  • Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio and Aaron Courville, Deep Learning, Chapter 3: Probability and Information Theory (2016). A compact reference for everything here, in the notation of ML papers.
  • David MacKay, Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms (2003). Free online. The best long treatment of entropy, coding and inference for people who like worked examples.
  • Christopher Olah, Visual Information Theory (2015). Entropy, cross-entropy and KL drawn as code lengths. The clearest pictures of the asymmetry of KL.
  • 3Blue1Brown, But what is the Central Limit Theorem? (2023). A visual tour of why sums of dice turn into bell curves.
  • Evan Miller, Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations (2024). Standard errors, paired comparisons and clustered questions for LLM benchmarks; the natural sequel to this chapter's σ/√n.
  • John Schulman, Approximating KL Divergence (2020). A short note comparing per-sample KL estimators; the source of the always-positive r − ln r − 1 that GRPO uses.
  • Mark Chen et al., Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code (2021). The Codex paper; it introduces HumanEval and the unbiased pass@k estimator with its numerically stable product form.
