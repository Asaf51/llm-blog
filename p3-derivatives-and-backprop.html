Derivatives & Backprop: how a network finds its next step

Training asks one question billions of times: if I nudge this weight, how much does the loss move? The answer is a derivative. This chapter rebuilds the calculus you need from a programmer's finite difference, shows how backpropagation gets every derivative of a model at once for about the cost of running it, and builds a working autograd engine in about 45 lines of Python.

In this chapter
  • A derivative is what (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h settles on as the nudge h shrinks. For x2 at 3 the ratios go 7, 6.1, 6.01 and settle on 6. It tells you how strongly, and in which direction, an output responds to a small nudge of one input.
  • About ten rules cover every function in this book. The ones that matter later: σ′ = σ(1 − σ) is at most 0.25, tanh′ is at most 1, and ReLU′ is either 0 or 1.
  • The gradient ∇L collects one partial derivative per parameter. It has the same shape as the parameters (8,030,261,248 numbers for Llama 3.1 8B) and points uphill, so training steps the other way.
  • The chain rule says that the rates of a pipeline multiply (3 × 5 × 0.16 = 2.4), and where paths merge, their contributions add. Backpropagation applies it in reverse over a computational graph using four local rules. For one sigmoid neuron, ∂L/∂w1 = −0.355, and one step cuts the loss from 0.1425 to 0.0690.
  • Reverse mode costs a small constant times one forward pass, however many parameters there are. Finite differences would need 8 billion forward passes per gradient: about 36 hours per token on an H100, against 49 µs.
  • Gradient checking compares backprop with central differences. In FP64 the best agreement is about 10−12, at h ≈ 10−6. In FP32 it is only about 3×10−6.
  • Gradients are long products, so they vanish or explode. In a 20-layer sigmoid stack the gradient that reaches the input is 2.6×10−13 of its size at the top. Curvature caps the step size: on ax2, gradient descent diverges for η > 1/a. Functions such as round() and argmax have no useful slope at all.
  • Build it: microgradlab. You write a scalar autograd engine, gradient-check every op, train an MLP on two moons to the accuracy reported in this chapter, race it against a vectorized NumPy version, and make gradients vanish and explode on purpose.

The question learning asks

Primer 1 trained a model by walking downhill on its loss: find the slope, step the other way. It treated the slope as something you can look at. With one or two parameters you can draw the loss as a curve or a landscape and see which way is down. Llama 3.1 8B has 8,030,261,248 parameters (Chapter 6 counts them), and nobody can draw that. The question is still the same, though, asked once for each parameter: if I nudge this weight up a tiny bit, how much does the loss change, and in which direction?

The answer to that question for one weight is a derivative. The answers for all the weights together form the gradient. This chapter is about computing both exactly and cheaply. Figure P3.1 asks the question about one weight of a tiny model that you will take apart by hand in Backpropagation by hand.

Systems analogy: a profiler for the loss

A profiler tells you which functions the time goes to, so you know where to spend effort. The gradient is a profiler for the loss. For every knob it reports how much the loss moves per unit of nudge, and with which sign. The analogy stops at one point: a profile describes the whole run, but a gradient describes only small nudges away from the current settings. After every training step the settings change, so the report has to be recomputed. That is why training computes billions of gradients, one per step.

Nudge and measure

A programmer would measure the slope directly: run the function, nudge the input by a small h, run it again, and divide the change in output by the change in input. Here it is for f(x) = x2 at x = 3:

def f(x):
    return x ** 2

def slope(f, x, h):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h

for h in [1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    print(h, slope(f, 3.0, h))

# 1 7.0
# 0.1 6.100000000000012
# 0.01 6.009999999999849
# 0.001 6.000999999999479

The ratio is a finite difference: a change in output divided by the finite change in input that caused it. As h shrinks, the answers settle on 6. The stray digits at the end (…012, …849) are floating-point rounding in the subtraction. They come back to matter in Gradient checking.

From finite difference to derivative

The derivative is the number that the finite difference approaches as h goes to zero:

f′(x) = limh→0 f(x + h) − f(x)h limh→0 is read "the limit as h goes to 0". It means the value the expression gets arbitrarily close to as h shrinks. h itself never reaches 0, which would be a division by zero. f′(x) is read "f prime of x". The other common notation is df/dx, read "d f by d x": a tiny change in f divided by the tiny change in x that caused it.

For x2 you can take the limit by algebra. (x + h)2 − x2 = 2xh + h2, and dividing by h leaves 2x + h. At x = 3 that is exactly the 6 + h the code printed: 7 for h = 1 and 6.1 for h = 0.1. Let h go to 0 and you get f′(x) = 2x. The derivative is itself a function: it gives the slope at every x. At x = 3 it is 6. At x = −1 it is −2, so the curve falls there. At 0 it is 0, the bottom of the bowl. Figure P3.2 shows the same limit geometrically.

The tangent line: the best straight-line guess nearby

The line through (a, f(a)) with slope f′(a) is the tangent line. Close to a, it is the best straight-line approximation of the function:

f(a + Δ) ≈ f(a) + f′(a) · Δ Δ (delta) is a small but finite change. Example: f(x) = x2, a = 3, Δ = 0.1 gives 9 + 6 × 0.1 = 9.6, and the true value is 9.61. The error, 0.01 = Δ2, shrinks much faster than Δ does.

This approximation is why gradients are useful at all. For a small enough step, the change in the loss is predicted by slope × step. In Figure P3.1 the slope −0.355 predicts that raising w1 by 0.1 lowers the loss by 0.0355, and the real drop is 0.0324. For the large nudge of 0.5 the prediction is off by 60%. A derivative is a local promise, which is why learning rates are small. Curvature and the Hessian says exactly how small.

Key takeaway

A derivative is a sensitivity. Its sign says whether nudging the input up pushes the output up or down. Its size says by how much per unit of nudge. It is only a promise about small nudges. In code it is the limit of (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h.

In practice nobody takes limits by hand. A short table of rules covers every function in this book, and an autograd engine is little more than this table with one entry per operation. Three facts from Figure P3.3 come back later. First, σ′ ≤ 0.25. Chain 20 sigmoids and the gradient shrinks by a factor of at least 0.2520 ≈ 9×10−13 (When gradients misbehave). Second, a saturated sigmoid or tanh unit, one whose input is far from 0, has a slope near 0 and barely learns. Third, ReLU passes a gradient through unchanged or blocks it completely. Note also that σ′ and tanh′ are written in terms of the function's own output. The backward pass can therefore reuse the value the forward pass already computed instead of evaluating ex again.

Optional deep dive: why the product rule looks the way it does

Nudge x so that f changes by Δf and g by Δg. The product becomes (f + Δf)(g + Δg) = fg + f·Δg + g·Δf + Δf·Δg. The last term is a product of two tiny numbers, so it vanishes faster than the others as the nudge shrinks. What remains is "the change in g, scaled by f, plus the change in f, scaled by g". Divide by the nudge and you have f′g + fg′. Think of a rectangle with sides f and g: growing both sides adds two thin strips plus a negligible corner. The same "drop the product of two small changes" argument gives the power rule, since x2 = x·x has derivative 1·x + x·1 = 2x.

Partial derivatives and the gradient

A loss depends on many parameters at once. A partial derivative asks the nudge question for one of them while every other input is held fixed. It is written ∂f/∂x and read "partial f by partial x" (or "del f del x"). The curly ∂ is a reminder that other inputs exist and are being held still.

Take f(x, y) = x2 + 3y2. To get ∂f/∂x, treat y as a constant: the 3y2 term ignores nudges of x, which leaves 2x. In the same way ∂f/∂y = 6y. At (2, 1) these are 4 and 6. The programmer's check agrees: f(2.001, 1) − f(2, 1) = 0.004001, so the nudge slope is 4.001, and nudging y instead gives 6.003.

The gradient collects all the partial derivatives into one vector. For this f it is ∇f = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y) = (4, 6). The symbol ∇ is called "nabla" or "del", and ∇f is read "grad f". For a loss L with parameters θ (theta, the book's name for "all the parameters"), the subscript says what you differentiate with respect to:

∇θL = ( ∂L/∂θ1, ∂L/∂θ2, …, ∂L/∂θN ) N is the number of parameters. Each entry is computed with all the other parameters held at their current values.

The gradient points uphill

Now nudge all the inputs at once, by a small vector Δ = (Δx, Δy). For small steps each input contributes its own slope × its own step, and the contributions add: Δf ≈ ∂f/∂x·Δx + ∂f/∂y·Δy = ∇f · Δ. That is a dot product (Primer 2). For steps of a fixed length, a dot product is largest when the step points the same way as ∇f. So the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length, here √(42 + 62) = 7.21, is the slope in that direction. A step at right angles to the gradient leaves f unchanged, to first order. That direction runs along the contour line, the set of points where f has the same value.

Flip the sign and −∇f is the direction of steepest descent. Following it is exactly Primer 1's update, now written for a whole vector of parameters at once: θ ← θ − η·∇L, where η (eta) is the learning rate. With η = 0.1 one step takes (2, 1) to (1.6, 0.4), and f drops from 7 to 3.04.

The gradient has the same shape as the parameters

There is one partial derivative per parameter, so the gradient has exactly the shape of the parameters, entry for entry. For Llama 3.1 8B that is 8,030,261,248 numbers. In one layer the gate projection (one of the MLP's weight matrices, Chapter 4) is a 14,336 × 4,096 matrix of weights, so its gradient is a 14,336 × 4,096 matrix too, and entry [i, j] says how the loss responds to that one weight. Frameworks store each gradient next to its weight (PyTorch's .grad, Primer 5). Stored in BF16, the 2-byte float format of Chapter 2, the full gradient takes another 16.06 GB, one reason training needs much more memory than inference (Chapter 8 counts all 16 bytes per parameter).

The chain rule: rates multiply

A network is a function of a function of a function. Each layer's output is the next layer's input, and the loss sits at the end of the pipeline. Training needs to know how the end responds to a nudge at the start. Take a three-stage pipeline: x → u = 3x + 1 → v = u2 → y = ln v. Each stage turns a small change at its input into a small change at its output, multiplied by its local rate. So the rates multiply:

dydx = dydv · dvdu · dudx Each factor is a local derivative. It comes from one stage alone, via the rules table, and is evaluated at the current forward values. In words: the rate of a pipeline is the product of the rates of its stages. This is the chain rule.

At x = 0.5 the forward values are u = 2.5, v = 6.25 and y = 1.8326. The local rates are du/dx = 3, dv/du = 2u = 5 and dy/dv = 1/v = 0.16. Their product is 2.4. To check: y = ln((3x + 1)2) = 2 ln(3x + 1), whose derivative is 6/(3x + 1) = 6/2.5 = 2.4. A finite difference with h = 0.001 gives 2.3986.

Two details of Step 6 matter for everything that follows. First, the product can be taken in either order, and starting from the output end gives a useful intermediate at every stage: how y responds to v (0.16), to u (0.8) and to x (2.4). Second, each local rate needs the forward value at its own stage: u = 2.5 for the square, v = 6.25 for ln. So the forward values must be kept until the backward walk reaches them.

Systems analogy: gain stages

Think of an amplifier chain. Each stage multiplies the signal by its gain, so the end-to-end gain is the product: 3×, then 5×, then 0.16× gives 2.4×. The difference is that a network's gains depend on the operating point, the current forward values, so they have to be recomputed for every input. That is why a derivative is always "at a point". The analogy also warns you of the next problem: a long chain of gains slightly below 1 kills the signal, and a long chain of gains slightly above 1 makes it explode.

When paths split and merge, contributions add

If x influences y along two separate paths, a nudge to x travels down both, and the effects add. The total derivative is the sum over paths of the product of local rates along each path. Here is the smallest example. Write x2 as one multiply node whose two inputs both happen to be x. Nudging the first input changes the product at a rate equal to the second input, x. Nudging the second input gives rate x as well. Both inputs move together, so the total is x + x = 2x. You have rebuilt the power rule from "multiply" and "paths add".

A real network has an astronomical number of paths from a weight to the loss, so nobody adds them up one by one. Backpropagation never lists paths at all. It adds the contributions where paths meet, once per node, as the next section shows.

Optional deep dive: the sigmoid's derivative via the chain rule

Write σ(x) = 1/(1 + e−x) as a pipeline: a = −x (rate −1), b = ea (rate ea), c = 1 + b (rate 1), σ = c−1 (rate −c−2). The product of the rates is (−c−2)·1·e−x·(−1) = e−x/(1 + e−x)2. Since 1 − σ = e−x/(1 + e−x), this equals σ(1 − σ). At x = 2: σ = 0.8808 and 0.8808 × 0.1192 = 0.1050, which matches the table.

Key takeaway

In a pipeline, local rates multiply. Where paths merge, the contributions add. Every local rate is evaluated at the current forward values, so they must be kept. Multiplying the rates starting from the output gives every intermediate gradient for free.

Backpropagation by hand

Every computation is a graph

Any program that computes a number from its inputs with arithmetic and elementary functions can be written as a sequence of tiny operations, each with one or two inputs. Draw each operation as a node and each value as an edge, and you get a computational graph: a directed acyclic graph (DAG) that runs from the inputs to the loss. Here is the running example for the rest of the chapter. It is one neuron with a sigmoid, as in Primer 1's logistic regression, scored with a squared error:

L = ( σ(w1x1 + w2x2 + b) − t )2 x1 = 2.0 and x2 = −1.0 are the inputs. w1 = 0.6 and w2 = 0.4 are the weights and b = −0.3 is the bias. t = 1.0 is the target. Squared error keeps the graph small; the MLP later in the chapter uses Primer 1's log loss instead.

Written as straight-line code, one operation per line, it is a tape:

p1 = w1 * x1        #  1.2
p2 = w2 * x2        # -0.4
s  = p1 + p2        #  0.8
z  = s + b          #  0.5
a  = sigmoid(z)     #  0.6225
d  = a - t          # -0.3775
L  = d ** 2         #  0.1425

Compiler writers will recognize static single assignment (SSA) form: every value is assigned exactly once. An autograd engine records this tape while the forward pass runs: the operation, its inputs and its output value. PyTorch builds the tape fresh on every call, as your Python code executes, so loops and if statements simply produce a different tape each time.

Four local rules

Backpropagation walks the tape backward. It carries one number per value: the gradient of the loss with respect to that value, ∂L/∂v, often just called "the gradient of v". It starts from ∂L/∂L = 1. Each node receives the gradient of its output and turns it into gradients for its inputs, using only its own local derivative. That is the chain rule, applied one stage at a time. Four rules cover everything:

  • Add routes. For z = a + b, both local derivatives are 1, so each input receives a copy of the incoming gradient.
  • Multiply swaps. For m = x·y, ∂m/∂x = y. So x receives the incoming gradient times y, and y receives it times x. This is why the forward values must be saved.
  • Fan-out sums. A value used by several operations receives a gradient from each of them, and they add up. This is "paths add" from the chain rule.
  • A function scales. A one-input function such as σ, tanh, exp or a square multiplies the incoming gradient by its local derivative from the rules table.

The neuron, forward and backward

Figure P3.7 runs the whole neuron: forward left to right, then backward right to left, one rule per node. Step through it slowly once. Every gradient in every network is computed by exactly these moves.

Read the results like a profiler report. ∂L/∂w1 = −0.355 is negative, so raising w1 lowers the loss: the output is too low, and x1 = 2 is positive. ∂L/∂w2 = +0.177 is positive because x2 = −1 is negative, so raising w2 would push z down. The gradients for the inputs x1 and x2 are not used to update anything, since data is not a parameter. In a deep network, however, the "input" of a layer is the previous layer's output, and that gradient is exactly what flows on to the layer below. Note the pattern in Step 7 as well: a weight's gradient is the gradient arriving at its neuron times the input the weight multiplied. Chapter 8 scales this pattern up to whole matrices.

Common misconception

"Backpropagation is an approximation, or something different from the chain rule." It is the chain rule, evaluated in a clever order, and it is exact up to float rounding. Central differences with h = 10−6 on this neuron give ∂L/∂w1 = −0.35489383472, and backprop gives −0.35489383470: they agree to a relative error of 7×10−11, which is float rounding in the finite difference. The approximations in training come from elsewhere, for example from estimating the full-data gradient on a mini-batch (Primer 1).

Reverse mode: every gradient for the price of one pass

Now count the cost. The finite-difference way to get a gradient is to nudge each parameter in turn and rerun the model: N + 1 forward passes for N parameters. For Llama 3.1 8B that is 8,030,261,249 forward passes per gradient. One forward pass costs about 2N = 16 GFLOP per token (Chapter 6), so the total is about 1.3×1020 FLOP for a single token. At the H100's 989 TFLOP/s BF16 peak (Chapter 2) that is about 36 hours to get the gradient for one token. In BF16 the nudged losses would also round to the same value, as Gradient checking shows.

Backpropagation needs one forward pass and one backward pass. Every node's backward step does a fixed, small amount of work: a multiply does two multiplications, an add copies a number, a sigmoid does one multiplication by its saved a(1 − a). So the backward pass costs a small constant times the forward pass, however many parameters there are. Automatic-differentiation theory calls this the cheap gradient principle and proves it for any program: Baur and Strassen (1983) bounded the constant by 5 when counting arithmetic operations, and the survey by Baydin et al. (Further reading), citing Griewank and Walther's textbook, quotes "below 6, typically 2–3". For neural networks, backward costs about twice the forward (Chapter 8 shows why). That makes the total about three forward passes, or 6N ≈ 48 GFLOP per token: about 49 µs at the same peak. The ratio to finite differences is roughly N/3 ≈ 2.7 billion. This general technique is called reverse-mode automatic differentiation; backpropagation is its name in neural networks. It was described by Linnainmaa in 1970 and popularized for neural networks by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986.

Forward mode, and why it loses here

The chain rule can also be applied left to right. Forward mode carries, next to every value, its derivative with respect to one chosen input (its "tangent"), computed with the same local rules as the values flow forward. On the pipeline of Figure P3.5 you start with dx/dx = 1, then get du/dx = 3, dv/dx = 5 × 3 = 15 and dy/dx = 0.16 × 15 = 2.4. It is the same number with the product taken in the other order.

The difference is what one pass buys. A forward-mode pass gives the derivatives of every output with respect to one input. A reverse-mode pass gives the derivatives of one output with respect to every input. A loss is a single number that depends on 8 billion inputs, so reverse mode wins by a factor of N. Forward mode wins in the opposite case, few inputs and many outputs, and it needs no tape. Frameworks offer both, and PyTorch has a forward-mode API too (torch.func.jvp), but training always uses reverse.

Systems analogy: forward vs backward dataflow analysis

Compilers run dataflow analyses in both directions. Reaching definitions flow forward from each assignment; liveness flows backward from each use. Forward mode asks "where does this one input's influence reach?", once per input. Reverse mode asks "which inputs did this one output depend on, and how strongly?", like a backward slice in a debugger, and answers it for every input in one sweep. When there is one output you care about, the backward direction wins.

The price: memory

Reverse mode has one real cost. The backward rules need forward values: the multiply swaps them in, and the sigmoid reuses a. Everything the forward pass computes that some backward rule will need must stay in memory until the backward walk reaches it. Memory therefore grows with the length of the computation, not with the number of parameters: in the worst case it is proportional to the number of operations the function executes. For LLMs these saved values, the activations, are a large part of training memory. Chapter 8 measures them and explains activation checkpointing, which recomputes some values instead of storing them.

Key takeaway

Reverse mode gets the derivative of one output with respect to all inputs in one backward sweep, at a small constant times the forward cost: about 3× in total for neural networks. Finite differences and forward mode need one pass per parameter. The price of reverse mode is memory: the forward values must be kept for the backward pass.

An autograd engine in 45 lines

All of this fits in one small Python class, in the spirit of Andrej Karpathy's micrograd (Further reading). Each Value holds a number, its gradient, the Values it was computed from, and the local derivatives with respect to them. Those derivatives are computed at creation time from the rules table. backward() sorts the graph topologically and walks it in reverse, applying the chain rule. This is the complete engine used for every result in the rest of the chapter:

import math

class Value:
    """A scalar that remembers how it was computed."""
    def __init__(self, data, parents=(), local_grads=()):
        self.data = data            # forward value
        self.grad = 0.0             # dLoss/dself, filled in by backward()
        self._parents = parents     # the Values this one was computed from
        self._local = local_grads   # d(self)/d(parent) for each parent

    def __add__(self, other):
        other = other if isinstance(other, Value) else Value(other)
        return Value(self.data + other.data, (self, other), (1.0, 1.0))

    def __mul__(self, other):
        other = other if isinstance(other, Value) else Value(other)
        return Value(self.data * other.data, (self, other), (other.data, self.data))

    def __pow__(self, n):           # n is a plain number
        return Value(self.data ** n, (self,), (n * self.data ** (n - 1),))

    def exp(self):
        e = math.exp(self.data)
        return Value(e, (self,), (e,))

    def log(self):
        return Value(math.log(self.data), (self,), (1.0 / self.data,))

    def tanh(self):
        t = math.tanh(self.data)
        return Value(t, (self,), (1.0 - t * t,))

    def sigmoid(self):
        s = 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-self.data))
        return Value(s, (self,), (s * (1.0 - s),))

    def relu(self):
        return Value(max(0.0, self.data), (self,), (1.0 if self.data > 0 else 0.0,))

    def __neg__(self):         return self * -1.0
    def __sub__(self, other):  return self + (-other)
    def __radd__(self, other): return self + other
    def __rmul__(self, other): return self * other
    def __rsub__(self, other): return (-self) + other
    def __truediv__(self, other): return self * other ** -1

    def backward(self):
        order, seen = [], set()
        def visit(v):                       # depth-first, inputs first
            if id(v) not in seen:
                seen.add(id(v))
                for p in v._parents:
                    visit(p)
                order.append(v)
        visit(self)
        self.grad = 1.0                     # dL/dL = 1
        for v in reversed(order):           # every consumer before its inputs
            for p, local in zip(v._parents, v._local):
                p.grad += local * v.grad    # chain rule; += handles fan-out

Four details carry the ideas of this chapter:

  • The rules table lives in the constructors. __mul__ stores (other.data, self.data) as its local derivatives, which is "multiply swaps" literally. sigmoid stores s * (1 - s), computed from its own output.
  • p.grad += local * v.grad is the chain rule: the local rate times the incoming gradient. The += is the fan-out rule, because a value used twice is visited from two consumers and collects both contributions.
  • The topological order. A node may pass its gradient on only when that gradient is complete, that is, after every consumer has contributed. A depth-first post-order lists every node after its inputs; reversed, every node comes before its inputs. It is the same ordering make uses to schedule a build, walked backward.
  • Gradients accumulate. Because of the +=, you must reset every .grad to 0 before the next step. PyTorch has the same rule, which is why training loops call optimizer.zero_grad() (Primer 5).

Run it on the neuron of Figure P3.7:

Gradients of vectors and matrices

This engine makes one graph node per number. Real frameworks put a whole tensor in each node, so the rules have to be stated for vectors and matrices. Two facts carry over unchanged.

  • The shape rule. The gradient of a scalar loss with respect to any tensor has that tensor's shape.
  • The simplest case. For a dot product y = w·x = Σi wixi (Σi means "sum over every i"), each term is a multiply, and multiplies swap: ∂y/∂wi = xi. As a vector, ∂y/∂w = x. With w = (0.6, 0.4) and x = (2, −1): y = 0.8 and ∂y/∂w = (2, −1). In the neuron, ∂L/∂w = ∂L/∂z · x = −0.1774 × (2, −1) = (−0.355, +0.177): both weight gradients of Figure P3.7 in one line.

For a layer y = Wx, with W of shape [out, in] (Primer 2), the same rule applied entry by entry gives ∂L/∂Wij = ∂L/∂yi · xj. That is an outer product, every entry of ∂L/∂y times every entry of x, with exactly the shape of W. The gradient passed down to the layer's input is WT times the incoming gradient. Stack many tokens into a batch and these become matrix multiplies. Chapter 8 derives that picture, three matmuls per layer, and the 6N FLOPs per token that follow from it.

Low-level view: why nobody trains with scalar autograd

I timed the small MLP of Training a tiny MLP end to end (65 parameters, 200 points) on one laptop CPU; your times will differ, but the ratios should be similar. Each full-batch step builds a graph of 30,268 Value objects, and one forward-plus-backward takes 43–60 ms. A NumPy version with a hand-written backward takes 0.09 ms and produces the same gradients to within 3×10−16. That makes the scalar engine about 500× slower, and the gap grows with width: 280× at 4 hidden units and 1,200× at 64. The cause is the one Primer 5 explains for Python loops: per-number interpreter overhead (object allocation, attribute lookups, a tuple of local derivatives) instead of one SIMD loop or GEMM kernel per tensor. PyTorch records one node per tensor operation, not one per number, so its tape for a 32-layer model is small even though the model has 8 billion parameters.

Gradient checking

A bug in a backward rule does not crash anything. Training just goes a little worse, or diverges for reasons that are hard to trace. The standard defense is to compare the analytic gradient with a numerical estimate. Use the central difference, which nudges both ways:

f′(x) ≈ f(x + h) − f(x − h)2h The error terms proportional to h cancel between the two sides, so the leftover error shrinks like h2 instead of h. For x2 it is exact: ((x + h)2 − (x − h)2)/2h = 4xh/2h = 2x.

Choosing h: two errors pulling opposite ways

Shrinking h reduces the truncation error, the part of the curve the straight line misses. It also increases the rounding error. As h shrinks, f(x + h) and f(x − h) agree in more and more leading digits, and the subtraction cancels them. What is left is rounding noise of about ε·|f|, where ε (machine epsilon) is the relative gap between neighboring floats: 2−52 ≈ 2.2×10−16 in FP64 and 2−23 ≈ 1.2×10−7 in FP32 (Chapter 2). Dividing that noise by a tiny h magnifies it. Figure P3.9 measures both effects for tanh at x = 0.5.

The best h sits where the two errors meet. A rule of thumb puts it near ε1/3 for central differences (about 6×10−6 in FP64) and near √ε for forward differences (about 1.5×10−8), which matches the measured minima. In BF16, with ε = 2−7 ≈ 0.008, gradient checking is hopeless, which is the other reason finite differences could never train an LLM.

A gradient check, in practice

  1. Use FP64 everywhere: inputs, parameters and every intermediate.
  2. Use central differences with h ≈ 10−6, scaled up for inputs much larger than 1.
  3. Compare with a relative error, |gbackprop − gnumeric| / max(|gbackprop|, |gnumeric|). With h = 10−6, the three weight gradients of the neuron of Figure P3.7 give 7×10−11, 3×10−11 and 3×10−11, and every op of the engine above passes at about 10−10 or better at x = 0.7. In FP64, anything above about 10−6 deserves a look.
  4. Stay away from kinks. ReLU at x = 10−7 with h = 10−6 straddles the corner. The central difference returns 0.55 while backprop correctly says 1. That is a false alarm, not a bug.
  5. Sample, and keep the model tiny. Each checked parameter costs two extra forward passes, so check a random subset of entries on a small model. PyTorch ships this as torch.autograd.gradcheck, with defaults eps=1e-6, atol=1e-5 and rtol=1e-3, and it expects double-precision inputs.
Gotcha: check at an ordinary point

Checking at "nice" points such as 0 or 1 hides bugs. Suppose you wrote the derivative of x3 as 3x instead of 3x2. At x = 1 both give 3, and the check passes. At x = 0 both give 0. Check at random, unremarkable values such as 0.7 or −1.3.

Training a tiny MLP end to end

So far everything has been one neuron. Now take a real, if tiny, network: 2 inputs, a hidden layer of 16 tanh units, and 1 sigmoid output. That is a two-layer MLP (multilayer perceptron, Chapter 4) with 65 parameters: 2 × 16 first-layer weights, 16 biases, 16 output weights and 1 output bias. The data is "two moons", two interleaved half circles of 100 points each, with a little noise. They cannot be separated by a straight line, so Primer 1's logistic regression cannot classify them. The loss is the mean log loss from Primer 1; Primer 4 explains it as a cross-entropy. Here is the model and training loop, written with the engine above:

import random

H = 16
W1 = [[Value(random.gauss(0, 0.7071)) for _ in range(2)] for _ in range(H)]
b1 = [Value(0.0) for _ in range(H)]
W2 = [Value(random.gauss(0, 0.25)) for _ in range(H)]
b2 = Value(0.0)
params = [w for row in W1 for w in row] + b1 + W2 + [b2]     # 65 Values

def predict(x):                       # x = (x0, x1): a point in the plane
    h = [(W1[i][0] * x[0] + W1[i][1] * x[1] + b1[i]).tanh() for i in range(H)]
    return sum((W2[i] * h[i] for i in range(H)), b2).sigmoid()

def loss_fn(X, Y):                    # mean log loss (Primer 1)
    total = Value(0.0)
    for x, y in zip(X, Y):
        p = predict(x)
        total = total - (p.log() if y == 1 else (1 - p).log())
    return total * (1 / len(X))

# X: 200 points as (x0, x1) tuples, Y: their 0/1 labels (generator: Build it)
for step in range(1000):
    for p in params: p.grad = 0.0     # gradients accumulate: reset them
    L = loss_fn(X, Y)                 # forward: builds a 30,268-node graph
    L.backward()                      # backward: fills in all 65 .grad fields
    for p in params: p.data -= 1.0 * p.grad   # gradient descent, learning rate 1.0

For the numbers below I drew the 200 training points and the initial weights from NumPy's seeded generator, so that the vectorized version could start from identical weights. The weight scales match the snippet: standard deviation 1/√2 for the first layer and 1/√16 for the second. After 1,000 full-batch steps the loss falls from 0.647 to 0.0167. 199 of the 200 training points are classified correctly (99.5%), and so are 99.7% of 1,000 fresh points from the same distribution, which tests generalization (Primer 1). A NumPy version of the same model replaces the 30,268 scalar nodes with a handful of array operations, and its backward pass is the same four rules written for arrays:

def forward_backward(P, X, y):              # X: [n, 2], y: [n] of 0/1
    A1 = np.tanh(X @ P["W1"].T + P["b1"])        # [n, 16]; W1 is [16, 2]
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(A1 @ P["W2"].T + P["b2"])[:, 0]))   # [n]
    L = -np.mean(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))
    dz2 = (p - y) / len(y)                    # log loss through the sigmoid: p - y
    g = {"W2": dz2[None, :] @ A1,             # [1, 16]: multiply swaps in A1
         "b2": np.array([dz2.sum()])}         # fan-out: summed over points
    dZ1 = (dz2[:, None] * P["W2"]) * (1 - A1 ** 2)   # back through W2, then tanh'
    g["W1"] = dZ1.T @ X                       # [16, 2], the shape of W1
    g["b1"] = dZ1.sum(axis=0)                 # [16]
    return L, g

The neat p - y is the chain rule at work. With L = −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)], ∂L/∂p = −y/p + (1 − y)/(1 − p). The sigmoid's local derivative is p(1 − p). Their product simplifies to p − y: the prediction minus the label. Chapter 8 starts its backward pass from the softmax version of the same identity (Where the gradient starts: p − y). Started from the same weights, this function and the scalar engine agree on every gradient entry to within 2.7×10−16.

When gradients misbehave

Backpropagation multiplies local derivatives along every path. In a deep network the paths are long, and long products of numbers that are not close to 1 either vanish or explode: 0.9100 = 2.7×10−5, while 1.1100 = 13,781. Chapter 4 makes the same point about the forward signal through 32 layers. This section is about the backward signal.

Vanishing and exploding gradients

Each layer multiplies the gradient passing through it by roughly (the scale of its weights) × (the slope of its activation). The sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so even with sensibly scaled weights each sigmoid layer cuts the gradient by about 4. Figure P3.10 measures this in a 20-layer network. At the input of the sigmoid stack, the gradient is 2.6×10−13 of its size at the top, close to the 0.2520 ≈ 9×10−13 you would predict. The first layers receive essentially no signal, so they barely learn. This is what kept deep networks hard to train before about 2010 (Glorot and Bengio, Further reading).

The other curves show that the cure is to keep each layer's factor near 1. tanh, whose slope reaches 1, keeps 0.18 of the gradient over 20 layers with the same weight scale. Halve its weights, though, and it vanishes to 6×10−7. ReLU with weights at the He scale stays near 1 (1.9). Make them 1.5× too large and the gradient explodes to about 12,000×. In FP16, whose largest value is 65,504, a few more layers of that would overflow to infinity (Chapter 2). In training, explosions show up as loss spikes and NaNs. The book covers the fixes where they are used:

  • Initialization. Choose each weight matrix's scale so that the layer's factor is about 1: Glorot (Xavier) initialization for tanh, He initialization for ReLU. The variance argument behind both is in Primer 4.
  • Residual connections compute out = x + f(x), whose derivative contains a plain identity term that carries the gradient through any depth with factor 1 (Chapter 4). In the experiment above, adding h ← h + f(h) to every sigmoid layer turns 2.6×10−13 into 1.2.
  • Normalization layers keep activations at a consistent scale, so slopes stay away from saturation (Chapter 4).
  • Gradient clipping and warm-up cap the length of the gradient vector and start training with tiny steps while the scales settle (Chapter 8).

Dead ReLUs

A ReLU unit whose input is negative for every example outputs 0 and has slope 0. No gradient reaches its weights, so nothing can revive it: it is dead. A common cause is one large step that pushes its bias far negative. In the widget, set ReLU and η = 10. In five runs with different seeds, 5 to 12 of the 16 units died within 50 steps, and the loss stalled between 0.2 and 0.32 with 85–88% accuracy, about what a straight line gets. With η = 1 none died. Smooth activations such as GELU and SiLU keep a small slope for negative inputs; Chapter 4 discusses why they replaced ReLU in LLMs.

Plateaus and saddle points

A zero gradient does not mean a minimum. f(x, y) = x2 − y2 has ∇f = (2x, −2y) = (0, 0) at the origin, yet the origin is a minimum along x and a maximum along y: a saddle point. Near saddles and on flat plateaus, gradients are tiny and training crawls. Dauphin et al. (2014) argued that in high-dimensional networks most troublesome critical points are saddles rather than bad local minima. The slow stretch in the MLP's loss table is a mild plateau, and with η = 0.1 the widget sits between losses of 0.30 and 0.25 for hundreds of steps before the boundary starts to bend. Momentum and the noise of mini-batches help optimizers move past such regions (Chapter 8).

Common misconception

"Training slowed down, so it has found a minimum." A small gradient can mean a minimum, a saddle or a plateau. A loss that stops falling can also mean the opposite problem: steps so large that they overshoot back and forth, which is the topic of the next section. Look at the gradient's size and at the loss curve's shape before deciding which case you are in.

Curvature and the Hessian

The derivative says which way is downhill. It does not say how far the straight-line guess can be trusted. That is set by curvature, the second derivative f″(x): the derivative of the derivative, the rate at which the slope itself changes. For f(x) = 5x2, f′ = 10x and f″ = 10: the slope changes by 10 per unit of x. A straight line has f″ = 0. Curvature adds a correction term to the tangent-line formula:

f(a + Δ) ≈ f(a) + f′(a)·Δ + ½·f″(a)·Δ2 For x2 at a = 3 with Δ = 0.1: 9 + 0.6 + ½ × 2 × 0.01 = 9.61, which is exact because x2 has no higher-order terms. The larger f″ is, the sooner the straight-line prediction goes wrong.

Why curvature limits the learning rate

Run gradient descent on f(x) = a·x2 with a > 0, so that f″ = 2a. The update is x ← x − η·2ax = (1 − 2aη)·x. Every step multiplies the distance to the minimum by the same factor, 1 − 2aη, so the iteration converges exactly when |1 − 2aη| < 1, that is, when 0 < η < 1/a = 2/f″. At η = 1/(2a) a single step lands exactly on the minimum. Between 1/(2a) and 1/a each step overshoots and the iterates zigzag, but they still converge. At exactly η = 1/a the factor is −1 and x bounces between ±x0 forever. Above that, it diverges.

Many parameters: the Hessian

With many parameters, curvature becomes a matrix. The Hessian H has one entry per pair of parameters, Hij = ∂2L/∂θi∂θj: how the slope along θi changes when θj moves. For f = x2 + 3y2 from Figure P3.4 it is diagonal, H = [[2, 0], [0, 6]]: curvature 2 along x and 6 along y. Gradient descent uses one η for every direction, so it must respect the sharpest one: η < 2/6 = 0.33. The flat x direction would tolerate η < 1 but has to move at the shared rate. With η = 0.3 the distance shrinks by a factor of |1 − 0.3 × 2| = 0.4 per step along x and |1 − 0.3 × 6| = 0.8 along y. In general the limit is η < 2/λmax, where λmax is the curvature along the sharpest direction (the Hessian's largest eigenvalue; for a diagonal H it is simply the largest diagonal entry). This is the edge Primer 1 measured on its line fit: sharpest curvature 14.39, so η < 2/14.39 = 0.139. When the sharpest and flattest curvatures differ a lot, the loss is a long, narrow valley. That is the picture Chapter 8 uses to motivate momentum and Adam.

Worked numbers: the Hessian of an LLM

Llama 3.1 8B's full Hessian would have N2 = (8.03×109)2 ≈ 6.4×1019 entries, or about 129 exabytes in BF16. Nobody forms it. Small slices are practical, though. GPTQ (Chapter 13) uses the Hessian of one layer's squared output error, H = 2XXT, where X holds the layer's inputs, one column per calibration token. It is a din × din matrix. For the 4,096-wide projections that is 16.8 million entries (67 MB in FP32); for down_proj, with din = 14,336, it is 205.5 million (822 MB). GPTQ uses it to decide how to compensate for each rounding error. Products of the Hessian with a vector can also be computed without forming H, by running autodiff twice.

Key takeaway

The second derivative measures how fast the slope changes, so it measures how far a gradient step can be trusted. On a bowl with curvature f″, gradient descent converges only for η < 2/f″. With many parameters the sharpest direction of the Hessian sets the limit for all of them.

Where derivatives break

Backpropagation assumes that every operation has a usable derivative. Three kinds of operation in LLMs do not, and the book handles each one differently.

Kinks: ReLU at 0

At 0, ReLU's slope is 0 from the left and 1 from the right, so the derivative is not defined there. Frameworks simply pick a value: PyTorch returns 0 at exactly 0 (I checked). Mathematically, any value between 0 and 1 is a valid choice (a "subgradient"). The choice is harmless because inputs are almost never exactly 0. Kinks matter mainly for gradient checking, where a nudge that straddles the corner gives a false alarm.

Steps: round() has zero derivative almost everywhere

Quantization (Chapter 13) rounds weights to a coarse grid. round(x) is flat between the half-integers, so its derivative is 0 there, and at the jumps it is undefined. PyTorch returns 0 everywhere. If a training run puts round() in the forward pass, no gradient reaches the weights behind it, and they never move. Quantization-aware training fixes this with the straight-through estimator: in the backward pass it pretends that round() is the identity and passes the gradient through unchanged (Chapter 13). It is a deliberate lie that works because rounding moves a value by at most half a grid step.

Choices: argmax and sampling

argmax returns an index, the position of the largest score. For two scores (s, 0), the winner flips from item 2 to item 1 at s = 0 and does not change anywhere else. The derivative is 0 everywhere except at the flip, where it does not exist. PyTorch's argmax returns an integer tensor that carries no gradient. Whenever a model in this book has to learn a choice, it replaces the hard choice with a soft one that has a slope. Attention mixes values with softmax weights instead of looking up the single best key (Chapter 5). A mixture-of-experts router picks experts with a hard top-k, but it scales their outputs by softmax gate values, and the router learns only through those (Chapter 7). Sampling a token is a choice too, so the reward an answer earns has no slope with respect to the weights. Reinforcement learning (Chapter 10) differentiates the probability p of the sampled answer instead, using the chain rule through ln: ∇ ln p = ∇p / p, so ∇p = p·∇ ln p (the log-derivative identity). Weighting each sample's ∇ ln p by how good it was turns "make the good samples more likely" into gradients of log-probabilities, the same gradients pretraining computes.

Chapter summary

A derivative is a nudge ratio in the limit. A gradient is all of them at once, shaped like the parameters. The chain rule multiplies local rates along a path and adds over paths. Backpropagation applies it in reverse with four local rules, for a small constant times the cost of the forward pass. It is exact, it needs the forward values in memory, and it fails in predictable ways: long products vanish or explode, curvature caps the step size, and steps and choices have no slope.

Hints (open only when stuck)
  • Gradients are twice as large on the second call: you did not reset .grad to 0. The += accumulates across calls by design.
  • RecursionError in backward(): summing per-point losses builds a chain one node deeper per point, so the recursive DFS fails beyond roughly 1,000 points. Replace it with an explicit stack of (node, visited-children) pairs.
  • A gradient is off by a sign or a factor of 2: check __sub__, __rsub__ and __pow__. 1 - p must call __rsub__, and the local derivative of x ** n is n * x ** (n - 1), not n * x ** n.
  • The check fails only for relu: you are within h of 0. Move the point.
  • math domain error in log: the sigmoid saturated to exactly 0 or 1 in FP64. Clip p to [10−12, 1 − 10−12], or compute the loss from the logit with a stable softplus.
  • Training on two moons is painfully slow: develop with 50 points and 100 steps, and run the full 200 × 1,000 once at the end. That slowness is the measurement of milestone 4.

Stretch goals

  • PyTorch parity. Rewrite the neuron and the MLP with torch.tensor(..., dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True), call .backward(), and compare every .grad with your engine. Primer 5 introduces the API.
  • Forward mode with dual numbers. Write a Dual(value, tangent) class with the same rules and compute dy/dx of the pipeline in one forward pass. Count how many passes a full gradient of the 65-parameter MLP needs in forward mode (65) and in reverse mode (1).
  • Measure the curvature limit. Confirm on f = ax2 that gradient descent diverges just above η = 1/a. Then estimate the MLP's largest Hessian eigenvalue by power iteration on Hessian-vector products (a finite difference of two gradients), and compare 2/λmax with the learning rate at which your training starts to diverge.
  • Mini-batches. Train with batches of 16 points instead of the full 200 (Primer 1) and compare loss against steps and against wall-clock time.

Further reading

  • Andrej Karpathy, micrograd (2020). The tiny scalar autograd engine this chapter's engine is modeled on, with a small neural-network library on top.
  • Andrej Karpathy, Neural Networks: Zero to Hero (2022). Its first lecture, "The spelled-out intro to neural networks and backpropagation: building micrograd", builds the engine live in about two and a half hours.
  • 3Blue1Brown, The Essence of Calculus (2017). The best visual refresher on derivatives, the chain rule and limits.
  • 3Blue1Brown, Backpropagation calculus (2017). The chain rule through a small network, drawn step by step.
  • Christopher Olah, Calculus on Computational Graphs: Backpropagation (2015). A short, clear explanation of forward versus reverse mode as sums over paths.
  • Baydin, Pearlmutter, Radul & Siskind, Automatic differentiation in machine learning: a survey (2015, JMLR 2018). How autodiff differs from symbolic and numerical differentiation, forward and reverse mode, and the history.
  • Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, Learning representations by back-propagating errors (1986). The four-page Nature paper that made backpropagation the standard way to train neural networks.
  • Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, Deep Learning, Chapter 6: Deep Feedforward Networks (2016). Section 6.5 treats back-propagation and computational graphs in full generality.
  • Glorot & Bengio, Understanding the difficulty of training deep feedforward neural networks (2010). The measurements behind vanishing gradients in sigmoid networks and the "Xavier" initialization.
  • Dauphin et al., Identifying and attacking the saddle point problem in high-dimensional non-convex optimization (2014). Why saddle points, not local minima, are the usual obstacle in high dimensions.
