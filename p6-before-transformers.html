Language Models Before Transformers: from counting words to attention

A language model is a probability for the next word given the words before it. Before transformers, people computed that probability by counting, then with small neural networks that read a fixed window, then with recurrent networks that carry a running summary through a loop. Each step fixed the last one's worst problem and exposed a new one. This chapter walks that path with real numbers and ends exactly where Chapter 1 begins.

In this chapter
  • A language model gives P(next word | previous words). The chain rule multiplies these into the probability of a whole text, and generating text is sampling from them again and again.
  • An n-gram model is a table of counts. In a 20-word toy corpus, P(cat | the) = 2/6, and the model gives "the dog sat on the mat ." (never seen) the same probability, 1/36, as a sentence it did see.
  • You grade a model by its average cross-entropy on held-out text, or by perplexity = ecross-entropy. On Shakespeare's characters, a counting model goes from 65 (uniform guessing) to 6.19 at n = 5, then gets worse again: 34.2 at n = 10.
  • The sparsity wall: 74% of the word trigrams in held-out Shakespeare never occur in the 228,000 training words, and a 50,000-word vocabulary has 1.25×1014 possible trigrams. Kneser–Ney smoothing helps (character perplexity 4.89 instead of 6.19), but counts never learn that cat and dog are alike.
  • Neural language models give every word a learned vector so that similar words share what they learn. Bengio et al. (2003) cut test perplexity on the Brown corpus from 312 (the best n-gram) to 252. A recurrent network replaces the fixed window with a state ht = tanh(Wht−1 + Uxt + b) updated once per word. LSTM and GRU gates keep its gradients from fading.
  • Recurrence has two bottlenecks. The loop is serial: on my laptop GPU, 1,024 dependent steps took 1.86 ms even in NVIDIA's optimized cuDNN RNN, against 0.042 ms for the recurrence's arithmetic done as one matrix multiply. And the whole past has to fit in one fixed-size vector. Attention (2014) fixed the second, and the Transformer (2017) removed the loop.
  • Tokens, embeddings, a softmax over the vocabulary, cross-entropy and autoregressive sampling all carry straight over into Chapter 1.
  • Build it: ngram-to-neural: count n-gram models on TinyShakespeare and watch held-out perplexity turn up, add Kneser–Ney, then train a Bengio-style MLP and a GRU on words in PyTorch that beat the best count model (142 and 125 against 166), and time the serial loop against a parallel matmul.

What a language model is

Your phone's keyboard suggests the next word as you type. Speech recognizers once had to decide whether you said "recognize speech" or "wreck a nice beach". A translation system has to choose the more natural of two possible outputs. All three need the same thing: a number that says how likely a piece of text is.

A language model supplies that number one step at a time. Given the words so far, it returns a probability for every word that could come next. In the notation of Primer 4:

P(wt | w1, …, wt−1) Read it as "the probability that the next word is wt, given that the words before it were w1 to wt−1". The vertical bar means "given". For each context the model returns a whole distribution: one probability for every word in the vocabulary, all of them summing to 1.

One distribution per step is enough to score a whole text. The chain rule of probability (owned by Primer 4) says the probability of a sequence is the product of each word's probability given everything before it:

P(w1, …, wn) = Πt=1n P(wt | w1, …, wt−1) Π means "multiply over t = 1 to n", the way Σ means "add". The first factor conditions on nothing, or on a start-of-text marker.

Figure P6.1 shows the rule on the book's running sentence, with the real probabilities that Llama 3.1 8B assigns to each token (the same numbers as Figure 1.9 in Chapter 1). Seven factors multiply to about 2.5×10−10. That looks tiny, but almost every specific seven-token text has a tiny probability. The useful comparisons are relative. Swap the nouns to get "The mat sat on the cat." and a good model should score it far lower. Working in logs turns the product into a sum, here 31.9 bits, and avoids floating-point underflow (Primer 4 explains why).

Turn the same machinery around and you can generate text. Draw a word from the next-word distribution, append it, and ask again. That is the generation loop of Chapter 1. Sampling one word at a time from the conditionals is exactly the same as sampling a whole text from the joint distribution the chain rule defines. Any model that can do the first job can do the second.

The definition does not care what a "word" is. In this chapter the unit is sometimes a word and sometimes a single character, because both are easy to count. Modern LLMs use tokens, pieces of words chosen by an algorithm (Chapter 3). Everything below applies to all three.

So the whole question is how to compute P(next | context). The rest of this chapter is the history of the answers. First people counted. Then they learned the probabilities with a neural network that sees a fixed window. Then they used a network that carries a summary of the past in a loop. Finally came attention, which can look back at every previous word directly.

Key takeaway

A language model is a conditional distribution over the next unit. The chain rule turns it into a probability for any text, and the loop of sampling one unit and appending it turns it into a generator. Every model in this chapter, and every LLM after it, is a different way of computing the same conditional.

Counting: n-gram models

The most direct estimate of P(sat | the cat) is to go through a lot of text and count. Divide the number of times "the cat" was followed by "sat" by the number of times "the cat" appeared at all. This works for short contexts. It fails for long ones, because almost every long context is unique. The 40-word sentence you are about to write has probably never been written before, so its count is zero.

The fix is to forget most of the history. An n-gram model assumes the next word depends only on the previous n − 1 words. With n = 2 (a bigram model) the context is one word, and with n = 3 (a trigram model) it is two. This shortcut is called the Markov assumption, after Andrey Markov, who in 1913 counted how vowels and consonants follow each other in Pushkin's Eugene Onegin. The probabilities are count ratios:

P(wt | wt−n+1 … wt−1) ≈ count(wt−n+1 … wt−1 wt)count(wt−n+1 … wt−1) The numerator counts how often the context was followed by wt. The denominator counts how often the context appeared. For a bigram model this is just count(prev next) / count(prev).

This ratio is the maximum-likelihood estimate. Of all possible tables, it is the one that gives the training text the highest probability. Primer 4 shows that maximizing likelihood is the same as minimizing cross-entropy, the loss every LLM is trained with. Counting gets to the answer in one pass, with no gradient descent, because for a table this simple the best answer has a closed form.

Here is a toy corpus of three sentences, 20 words in all, with the period counted as a word:

the cat sat on the mat . the dog sat on the rug . the cat saw the dog .

Figure P6.2 builds its bigram table. To keep the arithmetic tidy, the text wraps around, so the final period is followed by the first the. Every word then has a successor and the table has exactly 20 pairs.

The table does more than replay the corpus. "the dog sat on the mat ." never occurs in it, yet it scores 1 × 2/6 × 1/2 × 1 × 1 × 1/6 × 1 = 1/36, the same as "the cat sat on the mat .", which does. A bigram model recombines pieces it has seen: any path through the table is a legal sentence. That is a weak kind of generalization, and the only kind counting can give you. The flip side is also visible. "the mat sat" contains the pair mat → sat, which has count 0, so the model calls it impossible. It is also blind to everything before the previous word: after saw the it predicts exactly what it predicts after any other the.

Systems analogy: a hash table keyed by context

An n-gram model is a hash map from a context string to a row of counts. Training is one streaming pass that increments counters, the "word count" job from every MapReduce tutorial. Prediction is a lookup. A context the training text never contained is a cache miss: there is no row, and nothing tells you which stored key is "close". Keys are opaque strings. Keep that last property in mind, because it is the root of the problem two sections from now.

Counting scales well. Google's 2007 paper "Large Language Models in Machine Translation" (Brants et al.) trained 5-gram models on up to 2 trillion tokens, which produced up to 300 billion distinct n-grams spread over a cluster. The title is not an anachronism. Until the 2010s the biggest language models were tables of counts like this, and they were the language model inside speech recognizers, machine translation systems, spelling correctors and phone keyboards.

Generating from counts: Shannon's ladder

In 1948 Claude Shannon published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication", the paper that founded information theory. In section 3 he builds a "series of approximations to English" by sampling from n-gram models, by hand. For the zero-order approximation he drew letters uniformly from a book of random numbers. For the higher orders he used a trick any programmer will recognize as sampling from a table without building it. Open a book at random and pick a letter. Open another page and read until that letter appears, then record the letter that follows it. Repeat from the new letter. The book is the bigram table, and reading until a match is a lookup by linear scan. He did the same with letter triples and then with words, and stopped there because "the labor involved becomes enormous at the next stage".

Shannon noticed that each sample "has reasonably good structure out to about twice the range that is taken into account in its construction". The bottom half of Figure P6.3 repeats his experiment by computer, on the first 90% (1,003,854 characters) of TinyShakespeare, a public-domain file of Shakespeare's plays. At n = 3 you get English-shaped nonsense. At n = 5 the words are real and the punctuation is right. At n = 10 it reads like Shakespeare because much of it is Shakespeare: the median sample contains a stretch of about 38 characters lifted straight from the training text.

Common misconception

"Higher n is always better because the text looks better." Fluent samples can just mean memorization. A model that copies its training text verbatim has learned nothing that transfers to new text. To see that, you have to score text the model has not seen, which is the subject of the next section. Primer 1 calls this overfitting.

Measuring a language model: cross-entropy and perplexity

Looking at samples is a poor way to compare models. The standard test is to give the model text it was not trained on, called held-out text, and ask how much probability it assigned to what actually happened. A good model was rarely surprised.

The per-word number is the average cross-entropy (Primer 4 owns the concept): the mean of −ln p over every position of the held-out text, where p is the probability the model gave the word that really came next. Its exponential is the perplexity:

CE = −1N Σt=1N ln P(wt | contextt),    perplexity = eCE = P(w1 … wN)−1/N N is the number of predicted units in the held-out text. CE is in nats with the natural log; divide by ln 2 for bits. The last form follows from the chain rule: the average log-probability per unit is the log of the whole text's probability divided by N.

Perplexity has a concrete reading. A model with perplexity 6 is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 6 options at every step. A model that knows nothing about Shakespeare's 65 distinct characters and guesses uniformly has perplexity exactly 65. Lower is better, and 1 would be perfect prediction.

Worked numbers: the toy bigram model

Score "the dog sat on the mat ." with the table of Figure P6.2. It makes N = 7 predictions, and their probabilities multiply to 1/36. So the cross-entropy is ln(36)/7 = 3.584/7 = 0.512 nats (0.74 bits) per word, and the perplexity is 361/7 = e0.512 ≈ 1.67. The toy model is rarely in doubt: after sat, on, mat and the period it is certain. Now score "the dog saw the cat .". The pair dog → saw has count 0, so one factor is 0, ln 0 = −∞, and the cross-entropy is infinite. One unseen pair anywhere in a held-out book gives the whole book infinite perplexity.

Now real numbers. I split TinyShakespeare (1,115,394 characters) into the first 90% for counting, the next 5% for tuning and the last 5% (55,770 characters) as the test set. For each n from 1 to 10, I counted character n-grams on the training part. To avoid the zero problem I used add-k smoothing, explained in the next section, with k picked on the tuning part. Figure P6.4 shows perplexity on the test set and on the training text itself.

The two curves tell different stories. On the training text, perplexity keeps falling as n grows (27.2, 11.6, 6.8, 4.5, 3.6, … 2.8), because a longer context is always at least as informative about text you have already seen. On the test text it falls from 28.8 at n = 1 to a minimum of 6.19 at n = 5, then climbs to 34.2 at n = 10, worse than a model that ignores context entirely. The reason is visible in the counts. At n = 5, only 5.5% of the test positions have a 4-character context that never appeared in training. At n = 8 it is 37%, and at n = 10 it is 66%. For those positions the model has no row and falls back to a uniform guess over 65 characters. This is the classic overfitting curve of Primer 1: more capacity fits the training data better and the held-out data worse.

Caveat: perplexities only compare like with like

A perplexity is per unit, so it depends on the unit. Shakespeare averages 5.5 characters per word (counting the space), so 6.19 per character is the same total surprise as 6.195.5 ≈ 22,500 per word. Per token, it depends on the tokenizer. Compare perplexities only on the same test text with the same units. Chapter 8 uses perplexity for LLM training and explains the tokenizer caveat in detail.

The sparsity wall

Why not just count longer contexts on more text? Count the table. With a vocabulary of V words, there are Vn possible n-grams. For a modest 50,000-word vocabulary that is 2.5×109 possible bigrams and 50,0003 = 1.25×1014 possible trigrams. Most of them are nonsense ("mat the of"), but the sensible ones still far outnumber any training set. Each extra word of context multiplies the table by V while the amount of text grows only linearly, so the cells you have data for become an ever-smaller fraction of the cells you need.

Here is what that looks like on real text. I split TinyShakespeare into words (lower-cased, punctuation as separate tokens), with the first 90% of the file for training and the last 10% held out: 227,625 training words with a vocabulary of 11,687, and 25,974 held-out words. Then I asked how many of the held-out n-grams appear anywhere in the training text.

Already 4.4% of held-out words never appear in training. A word model cannot even name them. That problem alone is why modern models split text into subword tokens (Chapter 3). A third of the word pairs are new, three quarters of the triples and 94% of the 4-grams. The word mat does not occur in Shakespeare at all, so an unsmoothed trigram model trained on him gives "the cat sat on the mat" probability zero, and has no row at all for the context "the mat".

Smoothing and backoff

Zero counts make the loss infinite, so every practical n-gram model reserves some probability for events it has never seen. That is smoothing, and there are three classic ideas.

  • Add-k. Pretend every possible next word was seen k extra times: P = (count(context, w) + k) / (count(context) + kV). It is simple and never zero. It is also crude, because it hands unseen words the same small share whether they are plausible or absurd. All of the blue curve in Figure P6.4 uses it.
  • Backoff and interpolation. When the long context has little data, trust a shorter one. If "on the mat" was never seen, use what followed "the mat", or just how common mat is. Katz (1987) backs off only when a count is zero; interpolation always mixes all orders with weights.
  • Kneser–Ney. Kneser and Ney (1995) combined two ideas. Subtract a fixed discount (about 0.75) from every seen count and give the freed mass to the shorter context. And for the shorter context, count in how many different contexts a word appears, not how often it appears. Francisco is frequent, but almost only after San, so it should get little of the backed-off mass. Chen and Goodman's 1998 comparison found a modified Kneser–Ney the best smoothing method, and it stayed the standard until neural models.

Smoothing matters a lot. On the same character split as Figure P6.4, my interpolated Kneser–Ney model reaches 4.89 at n = 7, against 6.19 for the best add-k model. Because it backs off gracefully, its curve barely turns up (4.96 at n = 10). On words, where the wall is much closer, the gap is dramatic. With the vocabulary used in the next section (6,406 words), a trigram model scores a test perplexity of 1,423 with add-k and 166.3 with Kneser–Ney. For scale, the One Billion Word Benchmark (Chelba et al., 2013), a standard test of the 2010s, gives an unpruned Kneser–Ney 5-gram model a word-level perplexity of 67.6.

Optional deep dive: interpolated Kneser–Ney in one formula

For a context h, a next word w, and h′ (the context with its oldest word dropped):

PKN(w | h) = max(c(h w) − D, 0)c(h) + D · N1+(h •)c(h) · PKN(w | h′) D is the discount (0.5–0.9 in my runs, chosen on the tuning split). N1+(h •) is the number of distinct words seen after h, so the second term hands exactly the discounted mass to the shorter context. At the top order, c is the raw count. At every lower order, c(h w) is replaced by the continuation count N1+(• h w), the number of distinct symbols seen just before h w, and c(h) by the sum of those counts over all w. One D serves every order. If c(h) = 0 the model skips straight to PKN(w | h′). The recursion ends at a uniform distribution, so nothing is ever zero.

This is about 30 lines of Python with one hash map per order. The Build-it at the end of the chapter asks you to write it.

The deeper problem: counts do not generalize

Smoothing fixes the zeros but not the real problem. In an n-gram table, "the cat sat" and "the dog sat" are unrelated keys. Seeing "the cat sat on the mat" a thousand times teaches the model nothing about "the dog sat on the rug" or "a kitten sat on the carpet". Every fact has to be learned separately for every exact word sequence. The 228,000 training words of Shakespeare contain "the cat" once and "the dog" three times, so the counts for one cannot help the other, even though a human knows the two behave almost identically.

What is missing is a notion of similarity between words. Chapter 4 starts from exactly this observation about token IDs: "An ID is just a row number". Fixing it took a different kind of model.

Key takeaway

The number of possible contexts grows as Vn−1, so long contexts are almost always unseen: 74% of held-out Shakespeare trigrams never occur in training. Smoothing and backoff (best: Kneser–Ney) keep probabilities non-zero, but an n-gram model can never transfer what it learned about one word to a similar one.

Words as vectors: the neural language model

The fix is to stop treating words as opaque keys. Give every word a short list of learned numbers, a vector, and arrange training so that words used in similar ways end up with similar vectors. Then a model that has learned something about "the cat sat" automatically applies most of it to "the dog sat", because the inputs are nearly the same numbers. This is a distributed representation. The meaning of a word is spread across many coordinates, and each coordinate takes part in describing many words. Chapter 4 owns the geometry: what the vectors look like, and why similarity is a dot product.

Yoshua Bengio, Réjean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent and Christian Jauvin built the first successful language model on this idea, "A Neural Probabilistic Language Model" (2003; a first version appeared in 2000). It keeps the n-gram's fixed window of n − 1 previous words, but replaces the count table with a small neural network:

  1. Look up. Each context word selects one row of a learned table C of shape [V, m]: an m-number vector per vocabulary word. Selecting a row by ID is the embedding lookup (Chapter 3).
  2. Concatenate. Lay the n − 1 vectors end to end into one input vector x of length (n − 1)m.
  3. Hidden layer. Compute a = tanh(Hx + d): a matrix–vector product followed by a squashing nonlinearity (Chapter 4 owns linear layers and nonlinearities).
  4. Score every word. Compute one score (a logit) per vocabulary word, y = b + Ua. The paper optionally adds a direct term Wx.
  5. Softmax. Turn the V scores into probabilities that sum to 1 (Chapter 4).
P(wt | wt−n+1 … wt−1) = softmax(b + U tanh(Hx + d))wt,    x = [C(wt−n+1); …; C(wt−1)] C(w) is row w of the embedding table, [ ; ] is concatenation, h is the number of hidden units, H is [h, (n−1)m], U is [V, h], and b, d are bias vectors. The subscript wt picks the probability of the word that actually came next.

All of C, H, U, b and d are learned together by gradient descent on the cross-entropy of the next word. That is the loop of Primer 1, with gradients from backpropagation (Primer 3) and the LM loss that Chapter 4 defines. Nobody tells the model that cats and dogs are similar. If cat and dog are followed by similar words, the gradients pull their rows of C in similar directions, because that is the cheapest way to predict both well.

The model never stores a probability for a specific word sequence. It stores a smooth function, and similar inputs give similar outputs. So an unseen context gets a sensible distribution for free, with no smoothing rules at all. On the Brown corpus (about 800,000 training words, V = 16,383 after merging rare words) the best network (its probabilities averaged 50/50 with a trigram model's) reached a test perplexity of 252 against 312 for the best n-gram, a back-off model that also grouped words into 500 classes. The paper calls this a 24% difference: the n-gram's perplexity is 24% higher. On 14 million words of AP News the difference was 8%. The cost was compute. The paper reports about three weeks on 40 CPUs for 5 passes over AP News, almost all of it spent on the output layer: V scores, each a dot product of length h, for every single prediction. The same cost returns at LLM scale as a logits tensor too big to store (Chapter 8).

I ran the same recipe on the Shakespeare words of the previous section, with a 90/5/5 split and words seen fewer than twice mapped to one "unknown" token (V = 6,406). With 5 words of context, m = 64, h = 256 and dropout (Primer 1), the model has 2.14 million parameters and trains in a few minutes on a laptop CPU (12,000 steps of 256 examples). It reaches a test perplexity of 142.2. The best Kneser–Ney model scores 166.3 and the best add-k model 308.8, so the MLP is 14.5% lower than the best count model. The Build-it reproduces it.

word2vec: the vectors become the product

Ten years later, Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google asked a narrower question: what if you only want the vectors? word2vec (2013) throws away the hidden layer and most of the network. One variant predicts the surrounding words from a word's vector; the other predicts a word from the average of its neighbours' vectors. The models were cheap enough to learn high-quality vectors from 1.6 billion words in less than a day. They became famous for analogies: the vector for king − man + woman lands nearest to queen. Chapter 4 explains that result and its caveats, which are real.

For the history, the point is this. Pretrained word vectors became a standard input layer for other networks, and every LLM still begins with exactly this kind of table. Llama 3.1 8B's embed_tokens is a [128256, 4096] matrix: one 4,096-number vector per token, learned the way Bengio's C was.

The fixed-window limit

The neural model generalizes across similar words, but it still sees exactly n − 1 words. Anything earlier is invisible. In "The keys to the cabinet in the hall are missing", the verb has to agree with a noun seven words back. Widening the window makes the input vector, and the first weight matrix, grow linearly. Each position in the window also has its own slice of H, so a pattern learned at one offset does not transfer to another. Context length becomes a fixed architectural constant. What we want instead is a model that reads any amount of history with a fixed number of parameters.

Recurrent networks: a learned state machine

A programmer reading a stream does not keep a window of the last five items. They keep state: a running total, a parser's current mode, a checksum. Each new item updates the state, and the state is all you need to continue. A recurrent neural network (RNN) applies that idea to language. It keeps a vector h, the hidden state, and updates it once per word with the same learned function. Jeffrey Elman's "Finding Structure in Time" (1990) made this simple form popular:

ht = tanh(W ht−1 + U xt + b),    P(wt+1 | w1 … wt) = softmax(Vout ht + c) xt is the embedding of word t (length m), ht is the state after reading it (length H, the hidden size; from here on H is a size, not Bengio's matrix), and h0 = 0. W is [H, H] and mixes the old state, U is [H, m] and brings in the new word, b is a bias. Vout ([V, H]) and c turn the state into one score per vocabulary word.

Read it as a state machine whose transition function is learned. The parameter count does not depend on how long the text is. The same W, U and b are used at step 1 and at step 10,000, so in principle the prediction can depend on every word since the start.

Worked numbers: two steps of a 2-unit RNN

Take H = 2, W = [[0.5, −0.3], [0.2, 0.8]], U = [[1.0, −0.5], [0.3, 0.9]], b = 0, and toy embeddings x(the) = [1, 0], x(cat) = [0, 1]. Start from h0 = [0, 0].

Read "the". Wh0 = [0, 0] and Ux = [1.0, 0.3]. So h1 = tanh([1.0, 0.3]) = [0.762, 0.291].

Read "cat". Wh1 = [0.5·0.762 − 0.3·0.291, 0.2·0.762 + 0.8·0.291] = [0.293, 0.385], and Ux = [−0.5, 0.9]. The sum is [−0.207, 1.285], so h2 = tanh(…) = [−0.204, 0.858].

The second number in h2 carries part of "the" forward (through W) and part of "cat" (through U). After a hundred words, h is a blend of all of them, weighted by whatever W learned to keep. Matrix–vector products are Primer 2; tanh squashes each entry into (−1, 1).

To train it, unroll the loop. Draw one copy of the cell for each time step and connect each copy's output state to the next copy's input. The result is an ordinary feed-forward network, as deep as the text is long, in which every layer shares the same weights. Figure P6.7 shows it for the running sentence.

RNN language models took over from n-grams in the early 2010s. Tomas Mikolov's RNNLM (2010) reported about a 50% reduction in perplexity, for a mixture of several RNNs, against a state-of-the-art backoff n-gram model in speech recognition. Karpathy's 2015 essay "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks" made character-level RNNs famous by training them on the complete works of Shakespeare, Wikipedia, LaTeX and Linux source code. The 1.1 MB TinyShakespeare file used in this chapter is the sample dataset that ships with his char-rnn code.

My word-level runs on the same 90/5/5 word split and 6,406-word vocabulary as the sparsity section's Kneser–Ney numbers give these test perplexities. The best Kneser–Ney model is 166.3 (a trigram), the Bengio-style MLP with 5 words of context is 142.2, a plain tanh RNN is 142.5 and a GRU (a gated RNN, explained in the next section) is 124.8, 25% below Kneser–Ney. Both RNNs have H = 256, 128-number word vectors, dropout, and 2.6–2.8 million parameters, most of them in the output layer. Each neural model trained in under 15 minutes on a laptop CPU, and each picked its checkpoint on the tuning split. Fewer than a quarter of a million words is a small corpus by neural standards. With more data the gap widens, as the One Billion Word results in the next section show.

Caveat: tiny data and tiny alphabets favor counting

On characters the picture is different. With only 65 symbols the sparsity wall is far away: at n = 7 most contexts have been seen. There, my Kneser–Ney model (4.89) beat my best character-level MLP (5.46) and roughly tied my character GRU, which scored 4.95 against Kneser–Ney's 4.89 on the test split but 4.23 against 4.30 on the tuning split. Count-based models are strong baselines when the vocabulary is small or the data tiny. Neural models win where Vn explodes, which is words and subword tokens at scale.

Training through time, and why gradients fade

Training an RNN is backpropagation on the unrolled network, which in this setting is called backpropagation through time (BPTT; Werbos 1990 described it in detail). Primer 3 teaches backprop on small graphs, and nothing new is needed here. The loss at every position is a node at the top of the graph, and gradients flow back along the chain of states (the red arrows in step 5 of Figure P6.7). Because the same W appears in every copy, its gradient is the sum of its gradients from all time steps. That is the fan-out rule: a value used in several places collects a gradient from each.

Unrolling a whole book is impractical, so in practice training uses truncated BPTT. Cut the stream into chunks of, say, 64 or 128 steps. Carry the state h from one chunk to the next, but stop the gradient at the chunk boundary (in PyTorch, h = h.detach()). The model can still use information from long ago at prediction time. It just never receives a training signal telling it what to remember for longer than one chunk.

Products of many factors

The real problem is what happens to a gradient on its way back. To learn that keys should make the model predict are seven words later, the gradient of the loss at are must reach the state at keys. By the chain rule it passes through seven state-to-state steps and gets multiplied by each step's local derivative. In the scalar version (a state that is a single number, updated as ht = tanh(w ht−1 + …)), each factor is w · tanh′(·), the recurrent weight times the slope of tanh (at most 1). With vectors, each factor is a matrix, the Jacobian ∂ht/∂ht−1 = diag(1 − ht2) W: the table of partial derivatives of every entry of ht with respect to every entry of ht−1, where diag(v) is the matrix with v on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere. The story is the same. If the matrices shrink vectors, the signal from far back vanishes and the network cannot learn long-range dependencies. If they stretch vectors, the gradient explodes and one update can wreck the weights. Hochreiter (1991) and Bengio, Simard and Frasconi (1994) analyzed this problem. Primer 3 explains it for deep networks in general. Explosion has a blunt fix, gradient clipping: rescale the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold, which LLM training still does (Chapter 8). Vanishing needed a change of architecture. Figure P6.8 measures the effect on two trained models.

The plain RNN's gradient shrinks by about a third per step. Ten steps back, only 1.7% of the signal is left. Fifty steps back it is 9×10−8, which in practice means nothing gets learned at that distance. The gated network keeps a quarter of its signal after 50 steps.

LSTM and GRU: a protected memory lane

Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber's long short-term memory (LSTM, 1997) adds a second state vector, the cell state ct, and changes how it is updated. Instead of being rewritten through a matrix and a squashing function at every step, the cell state is updated by addition, under the control of gates. A gate is a vector of numbers between 0 and 1, computed by a sigmoid from the current input and state (Primer 1 introduces the sigmoid). It is multiplied element by element into a signal to let it through or block it.

ct = ft ⊙ ct−1 + it ⊙ gt,    ht = ot ⊙ tanh(ct) ⊙ is element-wise multiplication. gt is a candidate update, computed like an RNN's tanh step. The gates ft (forget), it (input) and ot (output) are each σ(W·[ht−1; xt] + b) with their own weights. The forget gate was added in 2000 by Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins; the 1997 cell simply kept its contents.

The key term is ft ⊙ ct−1. Along the cell state, the backward signal is multiplied only by the forget gate, not by a weight matrix and a tanh slope. When the network learns to hold f near 1 for some coordinates, those coordinates become a memory lane where information, and gradient, can travel for hundreds of steps. If you have read about residual connections in Chapter 4, this is the same trick: an additive path that the gradient can flow along unchanged. Figure P6.9 draws the two paths side by side.

The gated recurrent unit (GRU; Cho et al., 2014) is a lighter variant. It has no separate cell state, merges the forget and input gates into one "update" gate z, and interpolates between the old state and a candidate ĥt: ht = zt ⊙ ht−1 + (1 − zt) ⊙ ĥt. Where z is near 1, the old value passes through untouched. With three weight blocks per layer instead of the LSTM's four, it is about 25% cheaper and usually about as good.

Gated RNNs ruled language modeling from about 2013 to 2017. On the One Billion Word Benchmark, Jozefowicz et al. (2016) brought single-model perplexity down from 51.3 to 30.0 with large LSTMs (the Kneser–Ney 5-gram baseline was 67.6), and an ensemble reached 23.7. Machine translation and speech recognition moved to LSTMs in the same years, and phone keyboards followed: by 2018 Google's Gboard had replaced its n-gram next-word predictor with an LSTM variant (Hard et al., 2018).

Optional deep dive: the full LSTM and GRU equations

With zt = [ht−1; xt] (the previous state and the input, concatenated), an LSTM computes four blocks with one matrix multiply: it = σ(Wi zt + bi), ft = σ(Wf zt + bf), ot = σ(Wo zt + bo), gt = tanh(Wg zt + bg). Then ct = ft ⊙ ct−1 + it ⊙ gt and ht = ot ⊙ tanh(ct). That is why PyTorch's nn.LSTM stores weight_hh_l0 with shape [4H, H]: the four blocks are stacked. A common initialization sets bf to 1, so that the cell remembers by default.

A GRU computes an update gate zt = σ(…), a reset gate rt = σ(…), a candidate ĥt = tanh(Wxt + U(rt ⊙ ht−1) + b), and ht = zt ⊙ ht−1 + (1 − zt) ⊙ ĥt. Its weight is [3H, H]. Cho et al.'s paper and PyTorch's nn.GRU both use this form. Many tutorials swap z and 1 − z, which describes the same model with the gate's meaning flipped.

Key takeaway

Backprop through time multiplies one Jacobian per step, so gradients from far back shrink or blow up geometrically (0.950 ≈ 0.005). Clipping tames the blow-ups. LSTM and GRU gates add an additive, gated path through time, a protected memory lane, so useful signals survive for hundreds of steps.

Two bottlenecks: the loop and the fixed-size state

By 2016 gated RNNs worked well. Two limits remained, and neither could be fixed by tuning, because both follow from the update rule ht = f(ht−1, xt) itself.

Bottleneck 1: a loop-carried dependency

To compute h100 you need h99, which needs h98, and so on back to the start. In compiler terms, this is a loop-carried dependency: iteration t reads the result of iteration t − 1, so the iterations cannot run in parallel, however many cores you have. Within one step there is parallelism, across the batch and across the H hidden units. Each step is then one small matrix multiply, [B, H] × [H, H], followed by a barrier before the next step can start.

A model without recurrence has no such chain in training. The Bengio MLP's prediction at position 100 depends only on input words 95–99, never on its own earlier outputs. So all positions of all sequences in a batch can go through each layer together, as one large matrix multiply [B·n, d] × [d, d]. Chapter 2 explains why one big matrix multiply is so much faster than many small ones with the same FLOP count: more data reuse per byte loaded, full use of SIMD units or tensor cores, and one kernel launch (one function call on the GPU) instead of hundreds.

I measured it (Figure P6.10). Take one sequence of 1,024 steps with H = 256. Running the recurrence h = tanh(x[t] + h @ W.T) as a PyTorch loop took 9.8 ms on my laptop CPU (Intel Core i9-13900H, 14 threads, FP32). The same arithmetic, 0.13 GFLOP, done as one [1024, 256] × [256, 256] matrix multiply took 0.61 ms, 16× less. On the laptop's GPU (an NVIDIA RTX 4060 Laptop) the single matmul drops to 0.042 ms. The loop gets slower, 14.9 ms, because each of its 1,024 steps pays a fixed cost of about 15 µs to launch three tiny kernels from Python. NVIDIA's cuDNN library runs the whole recurrence in its own optimized GPU code, with no Python in the loop, and gets down to 1.86 ms. That is still 44 times the matmul. The usual workaround is a bigger batch, which gives each step more work. With 64 sequences of 128 steps, the loop costs about the same as the matmul on the CPU (1.1–1.3×) but is still 10× slower on the GPU (5× with cuDNN). A batch only adds width, and a GPU needs a great deal of width to fill thousands of cores (Chapter 2). The 2014 sequence-to-sequence system described in the next section needed 8 GPUs and about ten days to train.

Systems analogy: pointer chasing vs a vectorized scan

Summing an array is easy to vectorize. Walking a linked list is not, because you cannot load node t + 1 before node t tells you where it is. An RNN in training is the linked list: you cannot start on token t + 1 before token t's state exists. A transformer in training is the array scan: every position is independent within a layer, so the whole sequence becomes one big matrix.

Generation is serial for every model, RNN or transformer, because token t + 1 does not exist until token t has been sampled (Chapter 1). The difference is training and reading a prompt. A transformer processes a 1,000-token prompt in one parallel pass, called prefill, while an RNN must step through it 1,000 times (Chapter 11). Pretraining is nothing but reading, trillions of tokens of it, so the serial loop decided which architecture could use the hardware.

Bottleneck 2: the whole past in one fixed-size vector

The second limit is memory. Whatever an RNN knows about the text so far must fit in ht (and ct for an LSTM): a few hundred to a few thousand numbers, the same size after 10 words as after 10,000. My GRU's state is 256 numbers, 1 KiB in FP32. Every new word has to be written into that fixed space, which means overwriting something. The network learns a lossy compression of the past, tuned to what usually matters for the next word. A detail it decided to drop, a name mentioned three pages ago, a number to be copied exactly, is gone for good.

Compare the transformer that Chapter 1 starts from. It keeps a key and a value vector for every past token, in every layer: 128 KiB per token for Llama 3.1 8B, so 16 GiB for the full 131,072-token context (Chapter 12). Nothing is overwritten, and any old token can be read back exactly. It is the opposite trade: memory that grows with the context, against a state that stays fixed in size and forgets. In systems terms, an RNN state is a running hash of the stream: constant space and constant time per item, but item 5,000 cannot be recovered from it. A transformer keeps the append-only log and indexes into it, and Chapter 12's KV cache is that log, with the bill attached.

Sequence to sequence, and the attention fix

Translation is where language models met their hardest test. The output is a different sentence, in another language, of a different length and word order. In 2014 two groups published the same design, the encoder–decoder. Cho et al. (the paper that also introduced the GRU) used it to score phrase pairs inside a conventional translation system. Sutskever, Vinyals and Le ("Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks") showed that it could do the whole job on its own. One RNN, the encoder, reads the source sentence. Its final state is handed to a second RNN, the decoder. The decoder is a language model for the target language whose initial state is that summary, so it generates the translation word by word, feeding each output back in as the next input.

Sutskever et al.'s system shows the scale of the time. It used two 4-layer LSTMs with 1,000 cells per layer and 1,000-number word vectors, a 160,000-word input vocabulary and an 80,000-word output vocabulary: 380 million parameters. It trained for about ten days on an 8-GPU machine at about 6,300 words per second, and an ensemble of five reached 34.8 BLEU on the WMT'14 English→French test set (BLEU is the standard translation score, 0–100, computed from word overlap with human translations; a conventional phrase-based system scored 33.3). One of its key tricks reveals the bottleneck. Reversing the order of the source words helped a lot, because it put the first source words right next to the first target words and shortened the paths gradients had to travel.

Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio named the problem in their abstract: "the use of a fixed-length vector is a bottleneck". Their fix, "Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate" (2014), was to stop throwing away the encoder's intermediate states. At each output step the decoder computes a score for every encoder state, turns the scores into weights with a softmax, and reads the weighted average, a fresh context vector for that step:

αt,j = softmaxj( score(st−1, hj) ),    ct = Σj αt,j hj hj is the encoder state at source word j, st−1 is the decoder's state, score is a small learned network, and the weights αt,j over j sum to 1. The decoder reads ct along with its own state when it predicts output word t.

This is attention: a soft, learned lookup over a memory of vectors, with a weight for every entry instead of one exact hit. The decoder no longer has to carry the whole source in one vector. It can look back at any word, as often as it needs. Bahdanau et al.'s best attention model showed no drop in quality on sentences of 50 words or more, where the plain encoder–decoder had fallen off sharply, and their weight plots recovered word alignments that nobody had labeled. Two years later Google's production translation system, GNMT (Wu et al., 2016), used 8-layer LSTM encoders and decoders with attention. Google Translate switched to it for Chinese to English first.

The details of attention, queries, keys and values, scaling, masking and many heads, are Chapter 5's subject. Its "soft hash-table lookup" is exactly the step in Figure P6.11. The story of how the two halves, encoder and decoder, turned into the decoder-only models of today is Chapter 6's opening section.

2017: attention without the loop

By 2016, attention was doing the work of fetching the right information, and the RNNs were mostly there to produce the states it fetched from. Vaswani et al.'s "Attention Is All You Need" (2017) asked the obvious next question: why keep the RNN at all? Their paper states the problem in one sentence: the "inherently sequential nature" of recurrence "precludes parallelization within training examples".

The Transformer drops recurrence completely. Each position computes its new vector by attending to the vectors of all positions in the same sequence. That is self-attention: the same soft lookup as Bahdanau's, but over the sequence itself instead of over a separate encoder. Feed-forward layers then process each position on its own (Chapter 5 calls this the split between communicating and computing). There is no ht−1 in the update, so in training every position of every sequence goes through a layer at the same time, as the big matrix multiplies of the previous section. And any two positions are one attention step apart, however far apart they sit in the text.

Here n is the sequence length and d the vector width. The middle column is the parallelism argument. The right column is the gradient argument: a signal between word 1 and word 500 passes through one attention step, not 499 multiplications by a Jacobian. The left column is the price. Every position looks at every other, so the work and the score matrix grow as n2. That cost is the subject of a whole section of Chapter 5 (The n² problem). Attention is also blind to word order, so position information has to be added back (Chapter 5).

The payoff was immediate. The paper's base model reached state-of-the-art English→German translation after 12 hours on 8 NVIDIA P100 GPUs, and its big model trained in 3.5 days. Sutskever et al.'s LSTM had needed about ten days on 8 GPUs three years earlier. The bigger change was that the architecture finally matched the hardware. Training a transformer is almost entirely large matrix multiplies, the operation GPUs are built for (Chapter 2). Scaling up became a matter of buying more of them.

Common misconception

"Transformers replaced RNNs because they are better at language." Mostly they replaced RNNs because they train faster on parallel hardware: no serial loop over positions, and short gradient paths. What won was the ability to train far more parameters on far more data in the same wall-clock time. At generation time a transformer is just as serial as an RNN, one token per step. And, unlike an RNN, it pays for a cache that grows with every token (Chapter 12).

From n-grams to ChatGPT: a timeline

The Transformer was built for translation. Turning it into the LLMs of Chapter 1 took five more years and three more ideas: train on raw text first, make it bigger, then teach it to follow instructions. Figure P6.12 puts the whole path, from Markov to ChatGPT, on one line.

  • Pretrain, then adapt (2018). OpenAI's GPT-1 (June 2018) trained a 12-layer decoder-only transformer on next-token prediction over BooksCorpus, about 7,000 unpublished books, then fine-tuned it for each task. That is the language model of this chapter, scaled up and used as a starting point. Google's BERT (October 2018) took the encoder half and trained it to fill in blanks. It was excellent for classification and search, but it does not generate text fluently (Chapter 6).
  • Scale (2019–2020). GPT-2 (February 2019) had 1.5 billion parameters trained on WebText, about 8 million web pages, and could do some tasks with no fine-tuning at all. OpenAI first held back the largest version and released its weights in November 2019. Kaplan et al. (January 2020) measured that loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute. GPT-3 (May 2020) took that bet to 175 billion parameters and showed that a big enough model can pick up a task from a few examples in the prompt. Chapter 8 covers scaling laws and how the recipe has changed since.
  • Make it an assistant (2022). A pretrained model continues documents. It does not answer questions. InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., March 2022) fine-tuned GPT-3 on human demonstrations and then on human preference rankings with reinforcement learning (RLHF). Labelers preferred the 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT to the 175B GPT-3. ChatGPT, a sibling model trained the same way for dialogue, launched on November 30, 2022. Chapter 9 is about this stage.

Underneath all three, the object being trained never changed. It is the conditional P(next token | previous tokens) from the first section of this chapter, scored by cross-entropy on held-out text and sampled one token at a time.

Why recurrence came back

The fixed-size state was a weakness for recall, but it is also a strength for cost. An RNN spends the same time and memory on token 100,000 as on token 10. A transformer's per-token attention cost and its KV cache both grow with the context. Once contexts reached hundreds of thousands of tokens, that trade-off looked different. Since about 2020, researchers have found that attention with the softmax removed can be rewritten exactly as a recurrence with a matrix-valued state (linear attention). State-space models such as Mamba (2023) add input-dependent gates that decide what to keep, which is the LSTM's idea in a form that can be trained in parallel. Several models released from 2025 on mix many such recurrent layers with a few full-attention layers. Chapter 5's section on linear attention, state-space models and hybrids tells that story, and it contains the line "This is an RNN". You now know exactly what that means, and what it costs.

The mental model to carry forward

Every language model computes P(next unit | context). Counting does it with a lookup table and hits the sparsity wall. A neural network does it with learned vectors and shared weights, which generalize. A recurrent network reads any length of context, but through a serial loop and a fixed-size memory. Attention reads the whole context directly, and the Transformer runs that in parallel. Chapter 1 starts from the result: one function, tokens in and probabilities out, with its behavior stored in 16 GB of learned numbers.

  6. A recurrent model, its fading gradients and its serial loop

    Write the RNN language model with nn.RNN and nn.GRU (embedding 128, H = 256, dropout 0.3). Cut the training stream into 32 parallel streams and train on chunks of 64 steps, carrying h across chunks with h.detach() (truncated BPTT; AdamW at learning rate 3e-3 with weight decay 0.1 and cosine decay, gradient clipping at 1.0, about 2,000 steps). Evaluate statefully: one pass over the test stream with the state carried through. Then copy the trained weights into nn.RNNCell and nn.GRUCell, unroll 100 steps by hand with h.retain_grad(), and measure the gradient norm against distance. Last, time h = tanh(x[t] + h @ W.T) over 1,024 steps against the same FLOPs as one matmul.

Hints (open only when stuck)
  • Perplexity far below the reference. Your model can see the answer. The context window must end at position t − 1, not t. Check that a unigram model scores 28.8 and that your bigram model scores 12.1 with add-k.
  • Perplexity is inf. A zero probability slipped through, usually an unseen context with k = 0 or a Kneser–Ney level skipped incorrectly. Unseen contexts must fall back to a shorter one, or to uniform.
  • Kneser–Ney is worse than add-k. Lower orders must use continuation counts (distinct left neighbours), not raw counts, and the back-off weight must use the number of distinct followers of the context.
  • Word perplexities are not comparable to the reference. They depend on the vocabulary. Every model in the table must use the same <unk> mapping and score the same test tokens, including the first position of the test text.
  • The MLP overfits after a few thousand steps. Training loss falls while tuning perplexity rises. Use dropout, weight decay and a checkpoint chosen on the tuning split, as in Primer 1.
  • RNN training gets slower every step, or crashes with "Trying to backward through the graph a second time". You forgot h = h.detach() between chunks, so every step backpropagates through the whole history.
  • Timings jump around. Warm up first, take the median of many runs, fix torch.set_num_threads, and on a GPU call torch.cuda.synchronize() before reading the clock (Primer 5). Laptops change clock speeds, so a 2–3× spread between runs is normal for the loop.

Stretch goals

  • LSTM and forget-gate bias. Add nn.LSTM, initialize the forget-gate bias to 1 and to 0, and compare perplexity and the gradient-versus-distance curve with the GRU.
  • Bengio's mix. Average the MLP's and Kneser–Ney's probabilities 50/50, as the 2003 paper did, and check whether the mixture beats both. Then do the same on characters, where Kneser–Ney (4.89) is hard to beat on its own.
  • One attention layer. Replace the recurrence with a single causal self-attention layer over the last 64 words (Chapter 5 gives the exact computation). Compare its perplexity with the GRU and its training throughput with the loop.
  • Bits are bytes. Convert your best character model's test cross-entropy to bits per character (Kneser–Ney: 2.29) and compare it with gzip -9 (3.32), xz -9 (3.13) and bzip2 -9 (2.82) on the same 55,770 test characters. Further reading

  • Claude E. Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication (1948). Section 3, "The series of approximations to English", is the first n-gram language model, sampled by hand.
  • Dan Jurafsky and James H. Martin, Speech and Language Processing (3rd edition draft, free online). Its "N-gram Language Models" chapter covers smoothing, backoff and Kneser–Ney properly, and later chapters cover RNNs and transformers.
  • Yoshua Bengio, Réjean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent and Christian Jauvin, A Neural Probabilistic Language Model (2003). The paper that introduced learned word vectors for language modeling. Its introduction is still the clearest statement of the curse of dimensionality for text.
  • Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado and Jeffrey Dean, Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space (2013). word2vec: how to get good word vectors cheaply, and the analogy test.
  • Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber, Long Short-Term Memory (1997). The original LSTM, with an analysis of why plain RNN gradients vanish.
  • Christopher Olah, Understanding LSTM Networks (2015). The standard illustrated walkthrough of LSTM and GRU gates.
  • Andrej Karpathy, The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks (2015). Character-level RNNs on Shakespeare, Wikipedia and Linux source code. The TinyShakespeare file comes from the code released with it.
  • Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc V. Le, Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks (2014), and Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio, Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate (2014). The encoder–decoder, and the attention mechanism that fixed its bottleneck.
  • Ashish Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need (2017). Section 4, "Why Self-Attention", makes the argument of this chapter's last sections in two pages.
  • Andrej Karpathy, Neural Networks: Zero to Hero. A video course whose makemore lectures build a bigram model and the Bengio et al. MLP step by step, close to this chapter's Build-it, before moving on to a GPT.
