Pretraining

Pretraining turns about fifteen trillion tokens of text into the eight billion (or four hundred billion) numbers that make up a model. This chapter follows the whole process: how the data is built, what exactly is optimized, how gradients are computed, how the work is split over thousands of GPUs, and what it costs.

What pretraining is

A freshly built model is a stack of matrices filled with small random numbers. Its output is noise. Pretraining is the process that fills those matrices with useful values. The recipe is short: show the model real text, ask it to predict every next token, measure how wrong it was, and nudge every weight a tiny bit in the direction that would have made it less wrong. Then repeat this a million times over trillions of tokens.

Nobody writes rules into the weights. The only signal is "this was the next token". To predict well across code, math, law, recipes and chat logs, the model has to build internal machinery for grammar, facts, and some reasoning. Chapter 1 explains why next-token prediction can lead to capability. Here we look at the machine that does the nudging, and at its bill.

Intuition

Think of pretraining as fitting a giant lossy compressor. The training text is ~60 TB of token IDs. The result is a 16 GB file of weights (for an 8B model in BF16). The weights cannot store the text. They store the regularities that let the model guess the text well. A better guess means a lower "loss", which is exactly what training minimizes.

The data: from web crawl to trillions of tokens

The model can only learn what is in its data. Data work is the least glamorous part of pretraining and, by most accounts, the part that matters most. Two models with the same architecture and compute can differ a lot just because of data quality.

Where the text comes from

Most tokens come from the public web. The usual starting point is Common Crawl, a non-profit that publishes regular crawls of billions of pages, with the raw HTML stored as WARC files. On top of the web, labs add code (public repositories), books, scientific papers (arXiv, PubMed), Wikipedia, math sites, forums, and multilingual sources. Llama 3's data has a knowledge cutoff at the end of 2023. Some labs also license data or use their own products' public data; Meta, for example, says Llama 4 used public Facebook and Instagram posts.

The raw pool is enormous and mostly junk. The DCLM project re-extracted all Common Crawl data before 2023: 200 billion documents, 370 TB gzipped, about 240 trillion tokens. Their best filtered set kept 3.8T tokens, about 1.6%. Figure 8.1 shows the typical stages, with numbers from the open FineWeb pipeline.

Extraction and language ID

Common Crawl also ships pre-extracted text (WET files), but it keeps menus, cookie banners and footers. Better pipelines re-parse the raw HTML with a boilerplate remover (FineWeb used trafilatura; DCLM used resiliparse; Meta built its own parser that keeps math and code formatting). This step alone measurably improves the trained model. Then a tiny fastText classifier (a bag-of-n-grams linear model, microseconds per document) assigns a language. Llama 3 sorts documents into 176 languages this way.

Heuristic filters

Cheap rules remove obvious junk: pages that are too short or too long, have too many symbols per word, too few lines ending in punctuation, too many repeated lines or n-grams (log spam, error dumps), or contain "dirty words" missed by URL blocklists. These rules come from the Gopher and C4 papers and are tuned by training small models on filtered vs unfiltered data and comparing benchmark scores. Llama 3 also drops documents whose token distribution is far from the corpus average (measured by KL divergence, a standard distance between probability distributions), which catches pages full of outlier tokens.

Deduplication with MinHash

The web is full of copies: mirrors, templates, syndicated news, boilerplate licenses. Duplicates waste compute and make the model memorize. Exact duplicates are easy: hash each document (or each line) and keep one per hash. Llama 3 removes lines that appear more than 6 times in each bucket of 30M documents, which kills leftover navigation menus and cookie warnings. The hard case is near-duplicates: the same article with a different date line. Comparing every pair of 1010 documents is impossible (1020 pairs). MinHash with locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) solves it in near-linear time. Each document becomes a set of shingles (overlapping word 5-grams), and two documents A and B count as similar when their Jaccard similarity J = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|, the share of shingles they have in common, is high.

Why does the min-hash trick work? Apply a random hash to every shingle in A ∪ B. The shingle with the overall smallest hash is equally likely to be any element of the union. Both documents report that same minimum only if that element is in both sets, which happens with probability J. Each extra hash function is an independent coin flip with bias J. Banding then amplifies: a band of 8 hashes matches with probability J8, and at least one of 14 bands matches with probability 1 − (1 − J8)14. For FineWeb's settings that is 5% at J = 0.5, 77% at 0.75, and essentially 100% at 0.9. A systems person will recognize this as a hash join with deliberately "fuzzy" keys. FineWeb also found a subtle trap: deduplicating globally across all 96 snapshots made results worse, because it disproportionately kept low-quality leftovers; they deduplicate each snapshot separately.

Model-based quality filtering

After rules and dedup, the text is clean but often useless (product listings, SEO filler). The modern fix is a learned quality score:

  1. Label a sample. FineWeb-Edu asked Llama-3-70B-Instruct to rate 460,000 pages for "educational value" on a 0–5 scale. DCLM used "positive" examples from instruction data (OpenHermes 2.5) and the ELI5 subreddit, against random web pages as negatives.
  2. Train a cheap classifier on those labels (a small BERT-style model for FineWeb-Edu, a fastText model for DCLM) so it can run over billions of documents.
  3. Keep the top slice. FineWeb-Edu keeps score ≥ 3 (1.3T of 15T tokens). DCLM keeps the top 10%.

Llama 3 did the same with fastText (trained to recognize text "that would be referenced by Wikipedia") and RoBERTa-based classifiers trained on Llama 2's judgments, plus separate DistilRoBERTa classifiers that find math and code pages. A 7B model trained on DCLM-Baseline reached 64% on the MMLU benchmark with 2.6T tokens, close to Llama 3 8B's 66% with roughly 6.6× less compute. That is how much quality filtering buys.

Common misconception

"More data is always better." Not at a fixed compute budget. Every token you train on costs 6N FLOPs. Spending them on duplicated boilerplate is pure waste. The best pipelines throw away 95–99% of the crawl.

Decontamination, safety and PII

If benchmark questions leak into training data, scores become memorization tests. Decontamination removes training documents that share long n-grams with evaluation sets (8- to 13-gram overlap checks are common). Labs also drop domains with lots of personal data (PII) or unsafe content, and some anonymize emails and IP addresses (FineWeb does). None of this is perfect, which is why evaluation (Chapter 9) treats contamination as a standing risk.

The mixture

The cleaned sources are not used in proportion to their size. A data mixture sets the fraction of each batch that comes from each source. Llama 3's final mix was roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code, and 8% multilingual tokens. Mixtures are chosen by training small "proxy" models on candidate mixes and checking benchmark results, then confirming on a bigger model. Mixtures also change over time: Llama 3 increased the non-English and math share during training, and every modern run ends with a high-quality phase (covered later under annealing).

Synthetic data

High-quality human text is finite, so labs now generate more with existing models. Qwen3's report describes using earlier Qwen2.5 models to synthesize "trillions" of tokens of textbooks, question–answer pairs, instructions and code, and using a vision model to extract text from PDFs. Kimi K2 rephrases high-quality documents into new wordings to get more learning out of each fact. The risk is quality drift and homogeneity, so synthetic data is filtered like everything else.

How much data, and how it is stored

Modern runs use 15–36T tokens (table above). If you run out of unique data you can repeat it; studies of data-constrained training found that up to about four epochs of repetition is nearly as good as fresh data, with diminishing returns after that.

Low-level deep dive: the data loader

Tokenization (Chapter 3) is done once, offline. Llama 3's vocabulary has 128,256 entries, which does not fit in 16 bits, so token IDs are stored as uint32: 15T tokens × 4 B = 60 TB of binary shards. Documents are concatenated with an end-of-text token between them and cut into fixed-length rows (8,192 tokens for most of Llama 3's run). This packing wastes no compute on padding. Llama 3 adds a document mask so tokens cannot attend across a document boundary inside a row.

Bandwidth is not the problem. The 405B run consumed about 2.7M tokens/s across the whole cluster, which is only ~11 MB/s of token IDs. The real requirements are determinism and resumability: after a crash, every data-parallel rank must resume at exactly the right sample of a pre-shuffled stream, or the run silently trains on some data twice and some never.

Key takeaway

Pretraining data is a manufactured product: crawl → extract → filter → dedup → classify → decontaminate → mix → tokenize. The pipeline keeps a few percent of the raw web, and the choice of what to keep moves benchmarks as much as a large increase in model size.

The objective: predict every next token at once

Training needs one number that says how wrong the model is, so we can push it down. For language models that number is the cross-entropy loss: the negative log of the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next. Chapter 4 defines it: if the model gave the right token probability p, the loss is −ln p. This section shows how training computes it for a whole batch at once.

One sequence, n predictions

Here is the trick that makes pretraining efficient. Take a training row of n + 1 tokens. Feed the first n as input. The model outputs a probability distribution at every position, and the target at position t is simply the input token at t + 1. So one forward pass produces n predictions and n losses. The causal mask (Chapter 5) makes this honest: position t cannot see positions after t, so it cannot peek at its own answer.

This is called teacher forcing: every position is conditioned on the true previous tokens, not on what the model would have generated. The model never sees its own mistakes during pretraining. A systems analogy: it is one test run that executes n unit tests at once, where each test only has access to the fixtures before it.

Over a batch of B rows, the loss is the average over all B · n positions:

L(θ) = −1B·n ∑b ∑t ln pθ(xb,t+1 | xb,0 … xb,t) θ = all the weights. pθ(· | prefix) = the model's softmax output at position t. The sums run over the B rows and the n positions in each row. Nothing else is in the objective: no labels, no rules, just "make the real next token more likely".

Perplexity: the loss in friendlier units

Loss in nats is abstract. Perplexity is eL: the size of a uniform choice that would be equally uncertain. In Figure 8.3, the mean loss is 1.97 nats, so the perplexity is e1.97 ≈ 7.2: on average the model is as unsure as if it were picking fairly among about 7 tokens. Two anchor points:

  • At initialization the loss is ln 128,256 ≈ 11.76 nats (uniform guessing, Chapter 4), so the perplexity is 128,256. If your first training step does not show a loss near 11.8 for a Llama-3-sized vocabulary, something is wrong with the setup. This is a standard sanity check.
  • Bits per token = loss / ln 2. 1.97 nats = 2.84 bits. With arithmetic coding, a model with that loss could compress the text to 2.84 bits per token. This is the precise sense in which a language model is a compressor.

Perplexities are only comparable for the same tokenizer and the same test text. A model with a bigger vocabulary packs more text per token, so its per-token loss is not directly comparable to a model with a smaller one.

Where the gradient starts: p − y

Backpropagation (next sections) starts at the loss and works backwards. The first step has a beautiful closed form. With logits z, probabilities p = softmax(z) and a one-hot target y:

∂L∂zi = pi − yi For every vocabulary entry i: the gradient on its logit is "how much probability it got" minus "how much it should have got" (1 for the true token, 0 for the rest).

Worked example with a 4-token toy vocabulary and logits z = [2, 1, 0, −1], where the true token is index 1. Softmax gives p = [0.644, 0.237, 0.087, 0.032]. Loss = −ln 0.237 = 1.44. Gradient = [0.644, 0.237 − 1, 0.087, 0.032] = [0.644, −0.763, 0.087, 0.032]. Gradient descent subtracts the gradient, so the true token's logit goes up and every other logit goes down in proportion to the probability it stole. The gradient entries sum to zero, as they must.

Low-level deep dive: the logits tensor is huge

For one 8,192-token row and V = 128,256, the logits tensor has 8,192 × 128,256 ≈ 1.05 × 109 entries: 2.1 GB in BF16, 4.2 GB in FP32, and the same again for its gradient. Training frameworks therefore use fused, chunked cross-entropy kernels: compute the LM-head matmul for a chunk of positions, do softmax, loss and p − y in registers/shared memory, write only the gradient, and move on. The full logits tensor never exists in HBM.

Anatomy of one training step

A training run is one loop executed about a million times. Each iteration is a step, and each step has four phases:

  1. Forward. Run the batch through all layers, as at inference. Unlike inference, keep the intermediate results ("activations") that the backward pass will need.
  2. Loss. Compare the outputs with the targets (previous section).
  3. Backward. Walk the layers in reverse and compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight. Free each layer's activations once its gradients are done.
  4. Optimizer step. Update every weight using its gradient and the optimizer's running statistics. Reset the gradients to zero.
for step in range(num_steps):
    x, y = next(loader)               # [B, n] token ids; y = x shifted by one
    logits = model(x)                 # forward  -> [B, n, V], activations saved
    loss = cross_entropy(logits, y)    # scalar: mean over B*n positions
    loss.backward()                   # backward -> w.grad for every weight
    clip_grad_norm_(params, 1.0)      # cap the global gradient norm
    optimizer.step()                  # AdamW update using m, v state
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    scheduler.step()                  # next learning rate

Some real numbers for Llama 3 405B: each step consumed 16M tokens (in its main phase), which is 6 × 405 × 109 × 16.8 × 106 ≈ 4.1 × 1019 FLOPs. At the reported ~400 TFLOP/s per GPU on 16,384 GPUs, one step takes about 6 seconds, and 15.6T tokens / 16M tokens per step is roughly a million steps. The "6" in that formula is the subject of the next section.

Backpropagation

The problem: Llama 3.1 8B has 8,030,261,248 weights, and we need the derivative of the loss with respect to each of them. The naive way, nudging one weight and re-running the model to see how the loss changes, would cost 8 billion forward passes per step. Backpropagation computes all 8 billion derivatives for about twice the cost of a single forward pass.

The idea: the chain rule, run backwards

Any model is a graph of simple operations: multiply, add, ReLU, softmax, matmul. If you know how the loss changes with an operation's output, the chain rule tells you how it changes with the operation's inputs: multiply by the operation's local derivative. Start at the loss with "∂L/∂L = 1", walk the graph in reverse topological order, and every node gets its gradient exactly once. It is dynamic programming over the graph: the gradient of a shared intermediate value is computed once and reused by everything upstream, instead of being recomputed for each weight. (The technique's general name is reverse-mode automatic differentiation.)

You only need four local rules:

  • Add (z = a + b): copy the incoming gradient to both inputs.
  • Multiply (m = w · x): each input gets the incoming gradient times the other input's forward value. This is why forward values must be saved.
  • ReLU: pass the gradient through where the input was positive, block it where it was negative.
  • Fan-out: if a value feeds several consumers, its gradient is the sum of the gradients coming back from each. (Residual connections and weights shared across positions both rely on this.)

From scalars to matrices: three matmuls per layer

In a transformer almost all compute is linear layers. In the convention of Chapter 4, a layer computes Y = X·WT, where X is [n × din] (one row per token), W is [dout × din] and Y is [n × dout]. Applying the multiply rule entry by entry and collecting the sums gives two matrix formulas:

dX = dY · W        dW = dYT · X dY [n × dout] is the gradient arriving from above. dX [n × din] is passed down to the previous layer. dW [dout × din] has the shape of W; the product over the n dimension sums each weight's gradient over every token that used it (fan-out again).

Each of the three GEMMs costs 2 · n · din · dout FLOPs (Chapter 2's 2·m·n·k). Backward is two of them, so it costs twice the forward. In total, each weight costs 6 FLOPs per token: 2 forward, 4 backward. Worked example: Llama 3.1 8B's query projection Wq is 4,096 × 4,096 = 16.8M weights. On one 8,192-token row its forward GEMM is 2 × 8,192 × 4,096 × 4,096 = 2.75 × 1011 FLOPs, and training adds two more of the same size, 8.2 × 1011 in total.

Summed over the model, that gives the most useful formula in this chapter: training costs ≈ 6N FLOPs per token, versus 2N for inference (Chapter 6). The approximation ignores the attention score computations, which do not involve weights. Their forward cost is about 4·L·d·t at position t, which averages to 2·L·d·n per token over a causal row of length n, so 6·L·d·n with backward. For Llama 3.1 8B at n = 8,192 that is 6.4 GFLOP, about 13% on top of 6N = 48 GFLOP. At n = 131,072 it is 103 GFLOP, more than twice 6N, which is one reason long-context training is saved for the end of the run.

Low-level deep dive: how frameworks do it

PyTorch's autograd records every operation during the forward pass on a "tape": a graph of nodes, each holding pointers to the tensors it saved. loss.backward() walks the tape in reverse and launches each op's backward kernel. For linear layers these are ordinary cuBLAS GEMMs with transposed operands, so the backward pass runs on the same tensor cores at similar efficiency. Element-wise ops (SiLU, RMSNorm, residual adds) have memory-bound backward kernels; FlashAttention's backward recomputes the attention scores instead of storing the n × n matrix (Chapter 15).

Key takeaway

Backprop is the chain rule applied in reverse over the computation graph. It needs the saved forward values (memory) and costs about two forward passes (compute). Hence the two constants of training: 6N FLOPs per token and activations that must stay in memory until backward uses them.

Optimizers: from gradient descent to AdamW

The gradient tells you which way is uphill, locally. The optimizer decides how far to step and in exactly which direction. Picture walking down a mountain in fog with a flashlight that only shows the slope under your feet.

Gradient descent and SGD

w ← w − η · g w = a weight, g = ∂L/∂w (its gradient), η (eta) = the learning rate, a small number like 10−4 that sets the step size. Figure 8.5's update used η = 0.1.

The true loss is an average over all the training data, but nobody computes the full gradient. Each step uses a mini-batch (a few million tokens), which gives a noisy but unbiased estimate. That is stochastic gradient descent (SGD). The noise is not a bug: it lets training make progress after seeing a tiny fraction of the data, and it helps the optimizer avoid sharp, brittle minima.

Momentum: a heavy ball

Loss surfaces of neural nets have long, narrow valleys: very steep across, almost flat along. Plain gradient descent bounces from wall to wall and crawls along the floor. Momentum keeps a running velocity:

v ← β·v + g      w ← w − η·v β ≈ 0.9: how much of the old velocity survives each step. Components that flip sign every step (the bouncing) cancel out; components that point the same way every step (along the valley) add up, giving an effective step up to η / (1 − β) = 10η.

Adam: a separate step size for every weight

Different weights see gradients of wildly different sizes. The embedding row of a rare token gets a gradient once in a while; a normalization gain gets one every step. A single learning rate cannot suit them all. Adam keeps two running averages per weight and normalizes the step:

m ← β1m + (1−β1)g    v ← β2v + (1−β2)g2    w ← w − η m̂√v̂ + ε m = running mean of the gradient (momentum). v = running mean of the squared gradient (its typical size). m̂ = m/(1−β1k), v̂ = v/(1−β2k) correct for the zero initialization at step k. ε ≈ 10−8 avoids division by zero.

Worked example at step 1 with g = 0.5, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95: m = 0.05, v = 0.0125; bias-corrected m̂ = 0.05/0.1 = 0.5 and v̂ = 0.0125/0.05 = 0.25; step = η × 0.5/√0.25 = η × 1.0. With g = 0.0005 you get the same step, η. Adam's update size is set by η, not by the gradient's magnitude. The gradient mostly contributes a direction and a signal-to-noise ratio: weights with consistent gradients move about η per step; weights with noisy, sign-flipping gradients move less.

AdamW adds decoupled weight decay: w ← w − η·λ·w on top of the Adam step, which gently pulls every weight toward zero (λ = 0.1 is typical). "Decoupled" matters: adding the decay to the gradient instead would get divided by √v̂ and lose its effect. AdamW is the default for LLM pretraining. DeepSeek-V3 used β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, weight decay 0.1 and gradient clipping at 1.0, which are representative values.

Low-level deep dive: the optimizer step is memory-bound

AdamW does about a dozen FLOPs per weight but touches a lot of bytes: read the FP32 master weight, BF16 gradient, FP32 m and v (14 B), write back master, m, v and a fresh BF16 copy (14 B). That is ~28 bytes per parameter per step, or 225 GB for an 8B model. At 3.35 TB/s that is ~67 ms on one H100 if nothing were sharded, small next to a multi-second step. Frameworks use "multi-tensor" or fused kernels that update many tensors per launch, because launching one kernel per weight tensor would cost more in launch overhead than in work.

Optimizer research is still active. Kimi K2 (2025) was trained with MuonClip, a variant of the Muon optimizer that orthogonalizes each weight matrix's momentum update (via a few Newton–Schulz iterations) instead of scaling each entry separately, plus a clip that keeps attention logits bounded. Muon keeps only one state tensor per weight instead of Adam's two. AdamW remains the default.

Learning rate, batch size and stability

The learning rate is the most sensitive knob. Too high and the loss spikes or diverges to NaN. Too low and you waste millions of dollars of compute crawling. It is not constant: every run follows a schedule.

Warmup

Runs start with a linear warmup: the learning rate ramps from about zero to its peak over the first few thousand steps (8,000 for Llama 3 405B; 2,000 for DeepSeek-V3). At initialization Adam's v estimates are based on very few samples and the network is in a fragile state, so full-size steps early on cause blow-ups.

Decay: cosine and WSD

After warmup, the classic choice is cosine decay: the rate follows half a cosine wave from the peak down to a small floor at the planned end. Llama 3 405B used a peak of 8 × 10−5, decaying to 8 × 10−7 over 1.2 million steps. Its weakness: you must fix the total length in advance, and an intermediate checkpoint is never "finished".

Warmup–stable–decay (WSD) fixes that: hold the peak rate constant for most of the run, then decay quickly at the end (MiniCPM found ~10% of the tokens is enough). You can branch a short decay off any checkpoint to see how good the model would be if you stopped there, and keep training the main branch. Kimi K2 used WSD: constant 2 × 10−4 for 10T tokens, then cosine down to 2 × 10−5 over 5.5T. DeepSeek-V3 used a similar staged schedule: constant until 10T tokens, cosine decay over 4.3T, then two short constant phases at lower rates.

Why does lowering the learning rate drop the loss so fast? At a high rate the weights bounce around the floor of the valley, like the Adam path in Figure 8.7. Lowering the rate lets them settle toward the floor. This "annealing" effect is also why every run ends with a low-LR phase on the best data.

Batch size, in tokens

For LLMs the batch is measured in tokens: rows × sequence length. Bigger batches give less noisy gradients and more parallel work per step, but beyond a critical batch size doubling the batch no longer halves the number of steps needed, and the extra tokens are partly wasted. The critical batch size grows as the loss falls, so runs ramp the batch up:

  • Llama 3 405B: 4M tokens (rows of 4,096) → 8M (rows of 8,192) after 252M tokens → 16M after 2.87T tokens.
  • DeepSeek-V3: 3,072 → 15,360 rows of 4,096 tokens (about 12.6M → 63M tokens) over the first 469B tokens.
  • Kimi K2: a constant 67M tokens.

Gradient accumulation

If the batch is bigger than what fits in memory, split it into micro-batches: run forward and backward on each, add the gradients into the same buffer, and only then run the optimizer once. The math is identical to one big batch because the loss is an average. The global batch in tokens is:

tokens per step = micro-batch rows × accumulation steps × data-parallel replicas × sequence length Llama 3 405B, first stage: 32 rows per data-parallel group (fed through the pipeline as micro-batches) × 64 groups × 8,192 tokens = 16.8M tokens per step.

Gradient clipping and loss spikes

Occasionally a batch produces a huge gradient. Gradient clipping computes the global L2 norm of all gradients, ‖g‖ = √(Σ gi2) over every weight in the model, and if it exceeds a threshold (1.0 is standard), scales every gradient by 1.0/‖g‖. Direction is kept, length is capped. When weights are sharded across GPUs, this needs one extra all-reduce of the partial sums of squares.

Big runs still see loss spikes: the loss suddenly jumps and either recovers or diverges. Common causes are attention logits growing without bound, bad data batches, and too-high learning rates. Remedies include rolling back to a checkpoint and skipping the offending data, lowering the rate, normalizing queries and keys (QK-norm) or capping attention logits (Kimi's QK-Clip). Llama 3 reports "few loss spikes", DeepSeek-V3 "no irrecoverable loss spikes", and Kimi K2 shows a spike-free curve. Stability is a headline feature because a diverged run at this scale costs millions.

One more knob: initialization. Weights start as small random values (in GPT-2-style code, normal with standard deviation 0.02, with residual-output projections scaled down by depth). Getting the scale right per layer, so that hyperparameters tuned on small models transfer to big ones, is an active area; Meta's Llama 4 post calls its method MetaP.

Mixed precision: BF16, FP32 masters and FP8

Number formats themselves are covered in Chapter 2. Here is how training uses them, and why it needs more than one.

Why not everything in FP32?

Speed and memory. On an H100, BF16 tensor-core matmuls run at ~989 TFLOP/s; plain FP32 arithmetic runs at about 67 TFLOP/s. BF16 also halves the bytes moved. So the GEMMs, which are nearly all of the FLOPs, run in BF16 (with FP32 accumulation inside the tensor core).

Why not everything in BF16?

Because updates are tiny relative to the weights. BF16 keeps 8 significant bits, so near 1.0 the gap between representable numbers is 2−7 ≈ 0.0078. Take a weight of 1.0 and an update of −0.0001 (a learning rate of 10−4 times an Adam step of ~1): 1.0 − 0.0001 = 0.9999, which rounds right back to 1.0 in BF16. The update is lost, every step, forever (this is the "swamping" effect from Chapter 2). FP32's gap near 1.0 is 2−23 ≈ 1.2 × 10−7, so the update survives. Hence the standard recipe:

FP16 and loss scaling

Before BF16 hardware (pre-A100), mixed precision used FP16. FP16 has only 5 exponent bits: its smallest normal number is about 6 × 10−5. Many gradients are smaller than that: they lose precision, and below about 6 × 10−8 they become zero. The fix is loss scaling: multiply the loss by a large factor S (say 216) before backward, which scales every gradient by S and lifts them into range; then divide by S before the update. Dynamic loss scaling halves S and skips the step when an overflow (inf/NaN) appears, and slowly raises it otherwise. BF16 has the same 8-bit exponent as FP32, so it needs no loss scaling. That is the main reason BF16 won for training.

FP8 training

FP8 tensor cores double peak throughput again (H100: ~1,979 vs ~989 TFLOP/s dense). The difficulty is range: E4M3's largest value is 448, and one outlier forces a coarse scale on everything near it. DeepSeek-V3 made FP8 work at scale with three ideas:

  • Fine-grained scaling. Each 1 × 128 tile of activations (one token, 128 channels) and each 128 × 128 block of weights gets its own scale factor, so an outlier only hurts its own tile. They used E4M3 for all tensors.
  • Higher-precision accumulation. They measured that the H800's FP8 tensor-core accumulation keeps only about 14 bits, so partial sums are periodically promoted to FP32 registers on the CUDA cores.
  • Selective precision. The embedding, output head, MoE router, normalizations and attention stay in BF16/FP32, and master weights, weight gradients and optimizer states are kept in higher precision.

Their reported relative loss difference versus a BF16 baseline stayed below 0.25%. Meta reports pretraining Llama 4 Behemoth in FP8 at 390 TFLOP/s per GPU on 32K GPUs. FP8 training is now mainstream at the frontier.

Activations

Activations depend on how many tokens are in flight, not on N. A rough count for one Llama 3.1 8B layer, per token, in BF16, using FlashAttention (so no n × n score matrix is stored): the inputs of the two RMSNorms (2 × d), the normalized inputs to the attention and MLP projections (2 × d), Q, K, V after RoPE (d + d/4 + d/4 = 1.5d, thanks to GQA), the attention output (d), and in the MLP the gate, up and gated product (3 × dff):

(For comparison, Megatron's well-known estimate for GPT-style layers is 34·d bytes per token per layer, plus a term for the attention matrix that FlashAttention removes. It happens to give the same 139,264 B here.)

Activation checkpointing

Activation checkpointing (also called recomputation) trades compute for memory: store only each layer's input (2 × 4,096 B = 8 KiB per token per layer) and, during backward, re-run that layer's forward to regenerate the rest just before it is needed. Memory falls to 32 × 8 KiB × 8,192 ≈ 2.1 GB for the checkpoints, plus one layer's full activations (~1.1 GB) at a time. The price is one extra forward pass: 8N instead of 6N FLOPs per token, about +33%. Selective recomputation only regenerates tensors that are big but cheap to compute (attention internals, element-wise activations) and gets most of the savings for a few percent of extra compute. It is a classic space–time trade, like recomputing a value instead of caching it. Llama 3 405B, with its parallelism spreading activations over many GPUs, reports training 8K-token sequences without activation checkpointing.

Key takeaway

Training memory = 16 B × N for model states + activations that scale with tokens in flight. An 8B model needs ~128 GB of states; a 405B model needs ~6.5 TB. No single GPU comes close, which is why the next section exists.

Parallelism: splitting the job across thousands of GPUs

Two hard facts force distribution. First, memory: the 405B model's 6.5 TB of model states, against 80 GB per H100. Second, time: Llama 3 405B's 3.8 × 1025 FLOPs on one H100 at a realistic 400 TFLOP/s would take 9.5 × 1010 seconds, about 3,000 years. Spread over 16,384 GPUs it took a couple of months.

There are several independent ways to cut the work, and big runs combine them. Each cuts a different dimension and pays in a different kind of communication. The collectives they use (all-reduce, reduce-scatter, all-gather, all-to-all) are introduced in Chapter 2. The governing constraint is the network hierarchy: inside a server, 8 GPUs talk over NVLink at ~900 GB/s; between servers, each GPU typically gets one 400 Gb/s link (~50 GB/s), about 18× slower. Data parallelism

The simplest idea: give every GPU a full copy of the model and a different slice of the batch. Each computes gradients on its slice. Because the loss is an average, the full-batch gradient is the average of the slice gradients, so one all-reduce (sum, then divide) gives every GPU the exact full-batch gradient. All copies then apply the same update and stay identical.

Cost, worked out. A ring all-reduce makes each GPU send and receive about 2·(P−1)/P times the buffer size. For Llama 3.1 8B with BF16 gradients that is ≈ 2 × 16 GB = 32 GB per GPU per step. Over a 50 GB/s inter-node link: ~0.64 s. Now the compute: with 64 GPUs and a 4M-token batch, a step is 6 × 8.03 × 109 × 4.2 × 106 ≈ 2.0 × 1017 FLOPs, or about 7.9 s at 400 TFLOP/s per GPU. Communication is ~8% of the step, and frameworks hide most of it by all-reducing gradients in buckets as soon as each group of layers finishes its backward (Figure 8.4).

The weakness: every GPU stores all 16 bytes per parameter. As Figure 8.10 showed, that alone exceeds 80 GB for an 8B model.

ZeRO and FSDP: stop storing the same thing P times

In plain data parallelism, all P GPUs hold identical optimizer states, gradients and weights. That is pure redundancy, like every node of a cluster caching the full database. ZeRO (Zero Redundancy Optimizer) shards it in three stages. Let Ψ be the parameter count:

  • ZeRO-1 shards the optimizer states (FP32 master, m, v: 12 B/param). Each GPU updates only its 1/P of the weights. The all-reduce is replaced by a reduce-scatter (each GPU receives the summed gradient only for its shard) and, after the update, an all-gather of the new BF16 weights. Same total traffic as an all-reduce, which is itself a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather. Memory: 4Ψ + 12Ψ/P.
  • ZeRO-2 also shards gradients: 2Ψ + 14Ψ/P.
  • ZeRO-3 also shards the weights: 16Ψ/P. A layer's weights exist in full only while that layer runs: all-gather them just before its forward, free them right after, all-gather again for backward, then reduce-scatter its gradients. Traffic rises to about 1.5× plain DP.

For Llama 3.1 8B on 8 GPUs: 128.5 GB (plain DP) → 44.2 GB (ZeRO-1) → 30.1 GB (ZeRO-2) → 16.1 GB (ZeRO-3) per GPU. FSDP (Fully Sharded Data Parallel) is PyTorch's implementation of the same idea. Llama 3 used FSDP to shard optimizer state and gradients but kept the gathered weights after the forward pass, avoiding a second all-gather in backward. DeepSeek-V3 used ZeRO-1. Variants like ZeRO-Offload push optimizer state to CPU RAM, trading PCIe bandwidth for HBM.

Tensor parallelism

ZeRO splits storage, but each GPU still runs every layer on its own data. Data parallelism also runs out of road: you cannot grow the global batch forever (critical batch size), so beyond some GPU count each GPU's share of the batch becomes too small to be efficient. Tensor parallelism (TP) makes several GPUs cooperate on one layer by splitting its weight matrices.

The Megatron-LM trick needs only one all-reduce per MLP. Split the first matrices (gate and up projections, 4,096 → 14,336) by columns: each GPU computes a different slice of the 14,336 hidden units from the full input. SiLU and the element-wise product work per unit, so no communication is needed. Split the down projection (14,336 → 4,096) by rows, matching the slice each GPU already has: each GPU produces a full-size but partial output, and one all-reduce sums the partial outputs. Attention splits the same way along heads: each GPU owns some query heads (and their KV heads), and the output projection is row-parallel.

Why TP stays inside a node: per layer it moves 4 × 67 MB = 268 MB for one 8K-token row (2 all-reduces each way), on the critical path, between every pair of matmuls. Over NVLink that is cheap. Over a 50 GB/s network link it would stall the GPUs. A related trick, sequence parallelism, also splits the norm and residual regions between TP blocks along the sequence dimension; the all-reduce becomes a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather (same bytes) and the activations of those regions are sharded too. The same TP layout is used for inference (Chapter 11).

Pipeline parallelism

Pipeline parallelism (PP) puts different layers on different GPUs: stage 1 runs layers 1–8, stage 2 layers 9–16, and so on. Only the activations at stage boundaries travel, point to point: [tokens × d] per micro-batch, which is small enough to cross nodes. The problem is idle time. With one batch, stage 2 waits for stage 1, and in backward stage 1 waits for everyone. The fix is the CPU-pipeline trick: split the batch into m micro-batches so stages overlap. The remaining idle slots at the start and end are the bubble.

Bubble fractions: with p stages and m micro-batches, GPipe and 1F1B idle (p−1)/(m+p−1) of the time. The interleaved 1F1B schedule gives each GPU several non-adjacent chunks of layers (e.g., layers 1–4 and 17–20), cutting the bubble by that factor at the cost of more point-to-point traffic. Llama 3 used it and reports a bubble ratio of (PP−1)/(V·M) for V chunks per rank and M micro-batches. Newer schedules split the backward pass into its input-gradient and weight-gradient GEMMs (Figure 8.6) and slot the weight-gradient work into the bubbles ("zero-bubble" pipelines); DeepSeek-V3's DualPipe also feeds micro-batches from both ends of the pipeline and overlaps the MoE all-to-all with compute.

Context parallelism

Long rows break everything else: one 131,072-token row of Llama 3.1 8B needs about 4.46 MB × 131,072 ≈ 584 GB of activations. Context parallelism (CP) splits the sequence across GPUs. Every operation except attention is per-token and splits trivially. Attention needs keys and values from earlier chunks. Ring attention passes K/V blocks around a ring of GPUs while each computes attention for its local queries, overlapping transfer with compute. Llama 3 instead simply all-gathers K and V before attention, which is cheap with GQA because K and V together are 8× smaller than Q for the 405B model (8 KV heads vs 128 query heads). The causal mask makes later chunks more expensive, so Llama 3 cuts the row into 2 × CP pieces and gives rank i pieces i and 2·CP − 1 − i, balancing the work.

Expert parallelism

For mixture-of-experts models, expert parallelism (EP) places different experts on different GPUs and moves tokens to them with an all-to-all, then back again (Chapter 7 explains EP, routing and load balancing). In training, DeepSeek-V3 spread each layer's 256 routed experts over 64 GPUs on 8 nodes. Its full layout was PP = 16 × EP = 64 × ZeRO-1 DP, with no tensor parallelism at all.

Compute, MFU, time and money

Everything so far collapses into one estimate. Training compute is

C ≈ 6 · N · D C = total training FLOPs. N = parameters (for MoE, the active parameters per token). D = training tokens. The 6 is 2 (forward) + 4 (backward) FLOPs per weight per token. Attention-score FLOPs and recomputation are not included.

Llama 3.1 8B: 6 × 8.03 × 109 × 15 × 1012 = 7.2 × 1023 FLOPs. Llama 3 405B: 6 × 405 × 109 × 15.6 × 1012 = 3.8 × 1025, matching the paper.

MFU: how much of the GPU you actually use

Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) is the fraction of the hardware's peak that goes into the model's useful math: (model FLOPs per token × tokens per second) ÷ (number of GPUs × peak FLOP/s). Recomputation from activation checkpointing does not count; including it gives the higher "hardware FLOPs utilization". Good dense BF16 runs reach 35–45%; Llama 3 405B reports 38–43%. The other 55–65% goes to communication that is not hidden, pipeline bubbles, memory-bound kernels (norms, softmax, the optimizer step), kernel launch gaps, stragglers, data loading and checkpoint pauses. MFU is the efficiency number that labs fight over, because it converts directly into weeks and dollars:

time = CGPUs × peak × MFU     GPU-hours = Cpeak × MFU × 3600     cost = GPU-hours × $/GPU-hour

Worked example, Llama 3 405B. 3.8 × 1025 / (16,384 × 989 × 1012 × 0.41) = 5.7 × 106 s ≈ 66 days of pure compute, or ~26M GPU-hours. The model card reports 30.84M H100-hours, the gap covering phases at other configurations, restarts and everything not in 6ND. At a rental-equivalent $3.50 per H100-hour (October 2026 on-demand medians are roughly $3.4–3.6) that is about $90–110M of compute for the final run alone. Meta owns its hardware, so this is an illustrative market price, not what Meta paid.

Worked example, DeepSeek-V3. With 37B active parameters: 6 × 37 × 109 × 14.8 × 1012 ≈ 3.3 × 1024 FLOPs, about 11× less than Llama 3 405B despite similar token counts, because only 37B parameters are active per token. Things break: failures and checkpoints at scale

A cluster of 16,384 GPUs is a machine whose components fail daily. During a 54-day window of Llama 3 405B pretraining there were 466 job interruptions: 47 planned (firmware, config changes) and 419 unexpected, about 78% of them hardware. Top causes: faulty GPUs (148, 30.1% of unexpected), GPU HBM3 memory (72, 17.2%), software bugs (54), network switches and cables (35). Six were silent data corruption: a GPU returning wrong numbers without an error. Only three needed significant manual intervention; automation handled the rest, and the team kept effective training time above 90%.

The defense is checkpointing: periodically saving the complete training state (weights, optimizer state, data-loader position, RNG states) so a crash costs only the work since the last save. For the 405B model the model states alone are 6.5 TB. Llama 3's storage system (240 PB, 2 TB/s sustained, 7 TB/s peak) has to absorb those bursts with each GPU writing its own shard of 1 MB–4 GB in parallel.

The math: how often to checkpoint

419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days is one every ~3.1 hours. Checkpointing too often wastes time writing; too rarely wastes work on each crash. A classic first-order answer (Young's formula) is τ ≈ √(2·δ·M), where δ is the time to write a checkpoint and M is the mean time between failures. With δ = 1 minute and M = 180 minutes, τ ≈ √360 ≈ 19 minutes. Each failure then costs about τ/2 ≈ 10 minutes of lost work plus restart time, which is why fast restarts and asynchronous checkpointing matter as much as reliability.

Scale creates stranger problems too. Llama 3 saw a 1–2% throughput swing between day and night because midday heat lowered GPU clocks. And when tens of thousands of GPUs pause at once (say, waiting on a checkpoint or a collective), the data center's power draw swings by tens of megawatts, stressing the power grid.

Scaling laws

With a fixed compute budget C, should you train a bigger model on fewer tokens or a smaller one on more? Training many small models revealed a surprise: loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute, over many orders of magnitude. That makes the outcome of a giant run predictable from small ones.

Kaplan (2020)

OpenAI's Kaplan et al. fit laws like L(N) = (Nc/N)0.076 for loss versus (non-embedding) parameters, with similar laws for data and compute. Their allocation rule said to grow parameters much faster than data (N ∝ C0.73). GPT-3 followed that philosophy: 175B parameters on only 300B tokens, 1.7 tokens per parameter.

Chinchilla (2022)

DeepMind's Hoffmann et al. trained over 400 models from 70M to 16B parameters, this time tuning the learning-rate schedule to each run's length (Kaplan's runs had not). The answer changed: parameters and tokens should grow equally, about 20 tokens per parameter. To prove it, they trained Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) with the same compute as their earlier Gopher (280B, 300B tokens), and Chinchilla won across the board. They fit:

L(N, D) = E + ANα + BDβ E = irreducible loss (the entropy of text itself: even a perfect model cannot predict a coin flip). A/Nα = the penalty for a finite model. B/Dβ = the penalty for finite data. A 2024 replication by Epoch AI refit Chinchilla's data and got E = 1.82, A = 482, α = 0.348, B = 2,085, β = 0.366, which reproduces the ~20 tokens/parameter rule (the originally published constants imply a different ratio).

Minimizing L subject to C = 6ND gives Nopt ∝ Cβ/(α+β) ≈ C0.51. With the Epoch constants and Gopher's budget (5.76 × 1023 FLOPs) the optimum is ~72B parameters on ~1.3T tokens, almost exactly Chinchilla. The rule of thumb D = 20N turns C = 6ND into C = 120N2, so Nopt ≈ √(C/120): for 1024 FLOPs, about 91B parameters and 1.8T tokens.

Why modern models are "over-trained"

Chinchilla optimizes training compute only. But a model is trained once and then serves trillions of tokens, each costing ~2N FLOPs (Chapter 11). A smaller model trained far past its Chinchilla point reaches almost the same loss and is much cheaper to serve. Llama 3.1 8B saw 15T tokens: 1,870 tokens per parameter, ~90× the Chinchilla ratio. Under the Epoch fit, a compute-optimal model for the same 7.2 × 1023 FLOPs would have ~81B parameters and ~1.5T tokens, and its loss would be only ~0.05 nats lower (1.97 vs 2.02), while costing 10× more per generated token. For a model that will be deployed widely, that is an easy trade. The limit is data: high-quality tokens are finite, which is why synthetic data and repetition matter more each year.

Labs fit their own laws on their own data. Llama 3's fits (budgets from 6 × 1018 to 1022 FLOPs) predicted that 3.8 × 1025 FLOPs is best spent on 402B parameters and 16.55T tokens, about 41 tokens per parameter; they trained 405B on 15.6T. They also mapped loss to downstream benchmark accuracy, so the flagship's scores could be forecast before the run. Scaling laws are about loss, not abilities: specific skills can appear abruptly, and laws shift with data quality, architecture (MoE changes what N means) and training recipe.

Key takeaway

Loss is a predictable power law in N and D. For minimum training cost, use ~20 tokens per parameter (N ≈ √(C/120)). For minimum lifetime cost, train smaller models on far more tokens. That is what nearly every deployed model does today.

The final stretch: annealing and long context

The last few percent of pretraining are special. Two things happen there, often called mid-training.

Annealing on the best data

As the learning rate decays (Figure 8.8), the model settles, and what it settles on matters. Runs switch the mixture toward the highest-quality sources: curated math, code, reasoning, textbooks, sometimes instruction-like data. Llama 3 annealed during its final 40M tokens, decaying the learning rate linearly to zero while upsampling very high-quality sources, then averaged the checkpoints saved during annealing (Polyak averaging) to produce the final base model. Qwen3 has an explicit second stage of ~5T higher-quality, STEM- and code-heavy tokens with faster learning-rate decay. Because annealing has such a strong effect, Llama 3 also used it as a cheap data test: take a 50%-trained Llama 3 8B, anneal its learning rate to zero over 40B tokens with 30% of the mix drawn from a candidate dataset, and see how much the benchmarks move.

Long-context extension

Most training happens at 4K–8K tokens per row, because attention cost grows with sequence length and most documents are short. Long context is added at the end, gradually:

  • Llama 3 405B: six stages from 8K to 128K tokens, about 800B tokens in total (~5% of the run). A stage ends when short-context benchmarks have recovered and the model solves "needle in a haystack" retrieval at the new length. This stage is where the 16-way context parallelism in the table above was used.
  • DeepSeek-V3: two phases of 1,000 steps each, 4K → 32K → 128K, using YaRN position scaling.
  • Qwen3: hundreds of billions of tokens at 32K, raising RoPE's base frequency from 10,000 to 1,000,000.

The position-encoding side of this (RoPE scaling, YaRN, base changes) is explained in Chapter 5. The training side is what this chapter has covered: long rows mean huge activations (context parallelism), attention FLOPs that exceed the 6N term, document masks that prevent attending across packed documents, and data that actually has long-range structure (books, long papers, whole code repositories).

What comes out: a base model

When the run ends, the optimizer state is thrown away and the BF16 weights are exported (for Llama 3.1 8B, a 16 GB set of safetensors files; formats are in Chapter 6). That artifact is a base model.

A base model is a document completer. It continues whatever text it is given in the way such a document would most likely continue on the internet. Ask it a question and it may answer, or it may continue with three more questions, as if it were completing a quiz. It knows a lot and can do a lot (few-shot prompting works well), but it has no notion of a user, an assistant, turns, or when to stop and hand back control. It is also evaluated differently: by held-out loss and few-shot benchmarks rather than by chatting.

Turning this document completer into an assistant that follows instructions, uses tools and refuses harmful requests is post-training, the subject of Chapter 9. Teaching it to reason with long chains of thought using reinforcement learning is Chapter 10. Both cost a small fraction of pretraining's compute, but they decide how the model behaves.

Hints (open only when stuck)
  • Loss falls toward 0 within a few hundred steps: you forgot the shift, and the model is copying its input. Loss stuck near 10.8: your warmup starts at an LR of exactly 0 and never moves, or you call the optimizer on zeroed gradients.
  • Step-0 loss a bit above ln V: for roughly Gaussian logits with standard deviation σ, the expected loss is about ln V + σ2/2. Measure σ on a real batch and check that it explains the excess.
  • Accumulation mismatch: divide each micro-batch loss by the number of micro-batches (or weight by token count if rows differ in length), and clip and step only after the last micro-batch.
  • Not matching torch.optim.AdamW: it applies decay as a multiplication by (1 − η·λ) before the Adam update, adds ε to √v̂ after bias correction, and counts steps from 1. Match its parameter groups too.
  • Memory numbers look too big: memory_allocated excludes the CUDA context and the allocator cache, which is why nvidia-smi shows more. Under autocast, PyTorch also caches BF16 copies of the weights during the forward pass; those are transient, not model states. In amp mode the residual stream and RMSNorm run in FP32, which explains a good part of the activation excess.
  • Timing and FLOP counting: call torch.cuda.synchronize() before reading the clock, and discard the first ~10 steps (compilation, autotuning, allocator warmup). Count FLOPs in eager mode, then reuse the per-token number for compiled runs.
  • WSD branches: for each budget Di, save the constant-LR run at about 0.8·Di, then decay to near zero over the remaining 0.2·Di, and count the cooldown tokens inside the budget. Hägele et al. (2024, "Scaling Laws and Compute-Optimal Training Beyond Fixed Training Durations") use exactly this to cut the cost of scaling-law sweeps.
  • Exponent near Kaplan's 0.73, or a strange tokens-per-parameter ratio: check whether you counted embeddings in N and the LM head in C, and whether the short runs had a warmup that is too long for them. Pearce & Song (2024) and Porian et al. (2024) trace much of the Kaplan–Chinchilla gap to exactly these choices.
