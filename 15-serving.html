Serving at Scale

One forward pass is a solved problem. Running millions of them per second for thousands of users with different prompt lengths, tight latency targets and a budget is a scheduling, kernel and economics problem. This chapter covers how production engines do it, and how all of that turns into the price you pay per token.

In this chapter
  • Why continuous (iteration-level) batching beats batching whole requests, and what an engine's scheduler does every few milliseconds: queues, a token budget, KV-block admission and preemption.
  • How chunked prefill and prefill/decode disaggregation stop long prompts from freezing everyone else's token stream, and how many bytes of KV cache move between machines.
  • FlashAttention from first principles: tiling plus the online softmax (running max and running sum), worked with real numbers; then Flash-Decoding, kernel fusion and CUDA Graphs.
  • Speculative decoding: why a small draft plus one big verify pass gives the exact same output distribution, the expected-tokens formula, and when it stops helping. Includes a speedup calculator.
  • How the serving fleet is laid out: tensor, expert and data-parallel attention, the engine landscape in 2026, routing, autoscaling and cold starts.
  • Economics: deriving $/M tokens from GPU $/hour and throughput, why output tokens cost about 5× input, and what cached-input, batch and priority prices really buy you. Includes a cost and latency calculator.

The serving problem

Chapter 11 looked at one request: a prefill pass that processes the whole prompt in parallel and is compute-bound, then a decode loop that produces one token per step and is memory-bandwidth-bound because every step re-reads all the weights and the request's KV cache. That chapter also defined the latency metrics we use here: TTFT (time to first token), TPOT or ITL (time per output token, the gap between streamed tokens) and goodput (requests per second that meet their latency targets).

A real service is different in four ways.

  1. Many users at once. Hundreds or thousands of requests are in flight on one model replica. They arrive at random times.
  2. Wildly different shapes. One request has a 40-token prompt and wants 10 tokens back. Another has a 120,000-token prompt (a codebase) and will think for 8,000 tokens. You do not know the output length in advance.
  3. Latency targets, called SLOs (service-level objectives): for example "TTFT under 1 s for 99% of requests and TPOT under 50 ms". Chat users feel TTFT. Everyone feels a stream that stutters.
  4. Cost. An H100 rents for roughly $2–4 per hour in late 2026. Every idle millisecond is money. The provider wants the most tokens per GPU-second that still meet the SLOs.

The single most important fact from Chapter 11 is this: in decode, the GPU reads the full weights once per step no matter how many sequences share that step. Reading Llama 3.1 8B's 16.06 GB of BF16 weights on an H100 takes about 4.8 ms. If one user is decoding, that 4.8 ms buys one token. If 64 users share the step, it buys 64 tokens. So the whole game of serving is: keep as many useful sequences in every step as memory and latency allow, and waste nothing between steps. Everything in this chapter is a technique for one of those two goals, or for paying less per step.

Key takeaway

Decode cost is dominated by reading weights, and that read is shared by every sequence in the step. Serving is the art of filling each step with useful work without blowing the latency targets or running out of KV-cache memory.

Continuous batching: scheduling per iteration

The problem with batching whole requests

The obvious way to batch is the way you batch images for a vision model: collect B requests, run them together until they are done, return the results, take the next B. This is static or request-level batching. For LLMs it fails badly, for one reason: requests finish at different times. A batch runs until its longest member finishes. A request that wanted 5 tokens sits in its slot, finished, while its neighbor generates 500. Meanwhile new requests queue outside, waiting for the whole batch to drain. The GPU is busy, but much of the work in each step is padding.

The idea: re-form the batch every step

The fix, introduced by the Orca paper (Yu et al., OSDI 2022), is iteration-level scheduling, now usually called continuous batching. The scheduler makes a fresh decision before every forward pass: which sequences run in this step. A sequence that emits its end-of-sequence token leaves the batch immediately and its result is returned. A newly arrived request joins at the next step. The batch is a revolving door, not a bus that waits until the last passenger gets off.

Intuition

Think of a CPU's out-of-order core versus an in-order pipeline that stalls on the slowest instruction in a bundle. Or a thread pool with a work queue versus a fork-join that waits for the slowest worker. The granularity of scheduling drops from "request" (seconds) to "iteration" (milliseconds), so slots never sit idle while work is waiting.

In Figure 15.2 both schedules do exactly 63 token-steps of real work. Static batching spends 112 slot-iterations to do it (56% useful); continuous spends 80 (79% useful, and the remaining idle time is only because no work was queued). In production, with output lengths that vary from 1 to 10,000 tokens, the gap is much larger. The Orca paper reported up to 36.9× higher throughput than NVIDIA FasterTransformer on GPT-3 175B at the same latency, and every serious engine today (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, llama.cpp's server, TGI) is built on this idea.

The mechanics: one flat batch of tokens

How do you run one forward pass over sequences that are at different positions, with different lengths, some in prefill and some in decode? Orca's second idea, selective batching, answers this. Look at what each operation in a transformer layer needs (Chapter 6):

  • The linear layers (Q/K/V/O projections, the MLP, the LM head) and the norms are per-token. They do not care which sequence a token belongs to. So the engine concatenates every token being processed this step, from every sequence, into one flat matrix of shape [T, d], where T is the total token count. A decoding sequence contributes 1 row. A prefilling sequence contributes its prompt length. One big GEMM per weight matrix handles them all, with no padding.
  • Attention is the only operation that mixes tokens, and it must only mix tokens of the same sequence. So attention runs per sequence: each sequence's query rows attend to that sequence's own keys and values in the KV cache. Attention kernels take a small metadata array (the start offset and length of each sequence in the flat batch, plus each sequence's block table, see Chapter 12) and handle the ragged shapes internally.

So a step with 61 decoding sequences and one 450-token prompt chunk is a single forward pass on a [511, 4096] activation matrix for Llama 3.1 8B. The weight read is shared by all 511 rows. This "flatten everything, split only inside attention" layout is what makes continuous batching cheap.

Common misconception

"Batching makes each user slower." Partly true, but much less than you would think. In the memory-bound regime, going from 1 to 64 sequences adds only the KV-cache reads and a little compute to each step: for Llama 3.1 8B on an H100 with 2,048-token contexts, the step time roughly doubles (about 6 ms to 12 ms) while total tokens per step grow 64×. The trade-off curve is explored in the calculator at the end of the chapter.

The scheduler loop

Continuous batching moves the hard decisions into a scheduler that runs on the CPU between forward passes. Every few milliseconds it must answer: which requests run in the next step, how many tokens of each, and is there enough KV-cache memory for all of them? Here is the shape of the loop as implemented in vLLM's V1 engine (other engines are similar).

The state it keeps

  • A waiting queue of requests that have arrived but hold no GPU memory yet. Ordered first-come-first-served (FCFS) by default, or by a priority field.
  • A running list of requests that own KV-cache blocks and are being prefilled or decoded.
  • A KV block pool: all GPU memory left after the weights is carved into fixed-size blocks (vLLM uses 16 tokens per block by default) and handed out on demand, like pages in an OS. A request's KV cache lives in a list of blocks that need not be contiguous. The paging scheme itself, PagedAttention, and prefix caching belong to Chapter 12. Here we only need: a request can run only if it can get the blocks it needs.
  • Two limits: max_num_seqs (most sequences in one step) and a token budget, max_num_batched_tokens (most tokens in the flat batch of one step).

One iteration

vLLM V1 dropped the distinction between "prefill steps" and "decode steps". A schedule is just a dictionary {request_id: num_tokens}: how many new tokens of each request to push through the model this step. A decoding request asks for 1 (or more, with speculative decoding). A new request asks for its prompt length, or a chunk of it. This one representation covers continuous batching, chunked prefill, prefix caching (cached tokens are simply skipped) and speculative decoding.

# simplified from vLLM V1's scheduler (one call per engine step)
def schedule():
    budget = max_num_batched_tokens            # e.g. 8192
    plan = {}
    # 1) running requests first: they already hold memory and users are watching
    for req in running:
        n = min(req.tokens_left_to_compute, budget)   # 1 for decode, more for a chunked prefill
        while not kv.allocate_slots(req, n):         # need a new block?
            victim = running.pop()                   # lowest priority / newest
            kv.free(victim); waiting.push_front(victim)  # preempt: recompute later
        plan[req.id] = n; budget -= n
    # 2) admit waiting requests while budget and memory last
    while waiting and budget > 0 and len(running) < max_num_seqs:
        req = waiting.peek()
        cached = kv.get_computed_blocks(req)       # prefix-cache hit: skip these tokens
        n = min(req.prompt_len - cached, budget)     # chunk if the prompt is too long
        if not kv.allocate_slots(req, n): break     # out of blocks: stop admitting
        running.append(waiting.pop()); plan[req.id] = n; budget -= n
    return plan

# engine loop
while True:
    plan = schedule()
    logits = model.forward(flatten(plan))        # one pass, all requests
    tokens = sample(logits)                         # Chapter 14
    for req in finished(tokens): kv.free(req); stream_done(req)

Preemption, and why output length makes scheduling hard

The scheduler admits a request when its prompt fits, but it cannot know how long the answer will be. As sequences grow, the pool can run dry mid-flight. Then something must give: the scheduler preempts a running request, freeing its blocks. vLLM V1 recomputes the evicted request later (prefill is fast, and the prefix cache often still holds much of it); older engines could also swap KV blocks to CPU memory (Chapter 12 compares the two). Preemption wastes work, so it is a signal of overload: the vLLM docs suggest raising gpu_memory_utilization, lowering max_num_seqs or max_num_batched_tokens, or adding GPUs when it shows up in the logs.

The size of the KV pool, therefore, directly sets the maximum batch. For Llama 3.1 8B on an 80 GB H100 with about 55 GiB left for KV after weights and workspace (the vLLM budget worked in Chapter 11), 55 GiB ÷ 128 KiB per token ≈ 453,000 token slots, which is about 221 sequences at 2,048 tokens each, or 3 sequences at 128K. Long context is expensive in serving mainly because it shrinks the batch.

The CPU side matters too

A decode step for an 8B model on an H100 takes about 5–12 ms of GPU time. Scheduling, building input tensors, sampling bookkeeping, detokenizing and streaming are Python and CPU work that can take a meaningful fraction of that. If the GPU waits for the CPU between steps, utilization falls. Modern engines attack this from three sides: they run the API server, tokenizer and detokenizer in separate processes (vLLM V1's EngineCore runs only the scheduler and executor loop); they keep a persistent batch and send only diffs to the GPU workers; and they overlap scheduling of step i+1 with GPU execution of step i. SGLang calls its version the "zero-overhead batch scheduler": the CPU runs one batch ahead, using placeholder "future" tokens that are filled in when the GPU finishes.

Key takeaway

The scheduler is a memory allocator plus a packer. Each step: give running requests their next token first, admit new work while the token budget and free KV blocks last, run one flat forward pass, release finished requests. When memory runs out, preempt and recompute.

Chunked prefill: sharing each step between prefill and decode

The problem: one big prompt freezes everyone

Suppose 32 users are streaming answers, each receiving a token every ~8 ms. A new request arrives with an 8,192-token prompt. If the scheduler puts the whole prompt in the next step, that step must do 8,192 × 16 GFLOP ≈ 131.6 TFLOP of weight math plus about 18 TFLOP of attention. At ~60% of the H100's 989 TFLOPS that is about 250 ms. The 32 decoding users are in the same step, so each of their streams freezes for a quarter of a second. One long prompt every few seconds makes everyone's output stutter. This is prefill–decode interference.

The opposite policy, running decode-only steps until the queue is idle, starves the new request and ruins its TTFT. Neither extreme works.

The idea: cut the prompt into chunks and mix them with decodes

Chunked prefill, systematized by Sarathi-Serve (Agrawal et al., OSDI 2024), splits a long prompt into chunks and processes one chunk per step alongside the ongoing decodes. Each step gets a fixed token budget. Decodes are packed first (1 token each), and the rest of the budget is filled with the next chunk of a waiting prompt. Sarathi calls these stall-free schedules: new requests are added without ever pausing ongoing decodes.

This works because the two kinds of work stress different hardware. A decode step is memory-bound: the tensor cores are mostly idle while weights stream in. A prefill chunk is compute-bound. Put them in the same step and the chunk's math runs "for free" in the shadow of the weight read, up to the point where the step becomes compute-bound. Each chunk attends to the KV cache already written by previous chunks of the same prompt, so the result is identical to an unchunked prefill.

Choosing the budget

The token budget is the main knob, and it is a direct trade-off:

  • Small budget (say 512–2,048): short steps, smooth inter-token latency for running users, but a long prompt needs many steps, so its TTFT grows. Each extra step also re-reads all the weights, which costs a bit of efficiency.
  • Large budget (8,192+): prompts finish in fewer steps (better TTFT and throughput), but each step that carries a big chunk is long, so ITL spikes return.

vLLM enables chunked prefill by default in V1, schedules decodes before prefills, and its tuning guide recommends max_num_batched_tokens above 8,192 for throughput on large GPUs with smaller models, and smaller values like 2,048 when inter-token latency matters most. The roofline (Chapter 11) gives the rule of thumb: a BF16 step with T tokens does about T FLOPs per weight byte, and the H100's ridge point is ≈ 295, so a step becomes compute-bound at a few hundred tokens. Below that, extra tokens are nearly free; above it, each extra token makes the step proportionally longer.

Key takeaway

Chunked prefill turns "prefill or decode" into "how many prompt tokens ride along with this decode step". It caps the worst-case inter-token latency at the length of one budget-sized step and fills the idle tensor cores of memory-bound decode with useful prefill math.

Disaggregated prefill and decode

The problem chunking does not solve

Chunked prefill makes prefill and decode share a GPU politely. They still share it. That has three costs. First, interference never fully goes away: every step that carries a chunk is slower than a pure decode step, so ITL rises and wobbles with the prompt mix. Second, the two phases want different parallelism: prefill is compute-bound and likes enough GPUs per replica to finish long prompts fast, while decode is bandwidth- and memory-bound and likes huge batches and many GPUs' worth of KV memory. One configuration must compromise. Third, you cannot size them independently: if your traffic shifts toward long prompts with short answers, you cannot add "prefill capacity" alone.

The idea: two pools, connected by a KV-cache transfer

Disaggregated serving runs prefill and decode on different GPUs. A router sends the request to a prefill worker, which runs the prompt, produces the first token and the KV cache. The KV cache is then shipped over the network to a decode worker, which continues generation as if it had done the prefill itself. Each pool is configured, batched and scaled for its own phase.

How many bytes move?

The cost of disaggregation is the KV transfer. Use the per-token KV sizes from Chapter 12:

A 54 ms transfer is about 10% of that, and it can be hidden almost entirely by sending layer ℓ's K and V while layer ℓ+1 is still computing. The bytes are fine on modern RDMA fabrics. They are not fine over a slow datacenter network, which is why disaggregation needs GPUDirect RDMA (NIC reads GPU memory directly, no CPU bounce) or NVLink domains like NVL72 racks.

The systems

  • DistServe (Zhong et al., OSDI 2024) framed the goal as goodput under both a TTFT and a TPOT SLO, and searched for the best parallelism per phase. It reported up to 7.4× more requests, or 12.6× tighter SLOs, within the latency targets.
  • Splitwise (Patel et al., Microsoft, ISCA 2024) observed that token generation does not need the newest GPU's FLOPs and proposed heterogeneous clusters, e.g. prefill on the newest GPUs and decode on cheaper ones. It reported up to 1.4× throughput at 20% lower cost.
  • Mooncake (Moonshot AI's serving platform for Kimi, FAST 2025) is KVCache-centric: besides separate prefill and decode clusters, it pools the CPU DRAM and SSDs of the GPU servers into a distributed KV-cache store, so a prefix computed once can be fetched by any node, and it rejects requests early under overload. It reported handling 75% more requests on real workloads.
  • NVIDIA Dynamo (open source, March 2025) packages the pattern for vLLM, SGLang and TensorRT-LLM: a KV-aware router, a planner that rebalances prefill vs decode GPUs as traffic shifts, a KV block manager that offloads to cheaper memory tiers, and NIXL, a transfer library that moves KV blocks between GPU memory, CPU memory and storage over NVLink, InfiniBand, RoCE or Ethernet. llm-d (a CNCF sandbox project founded by Red Hat, Google Cloud, IBM Research, CoreWeave and NVIDIA) does the same on Kubernetes on top of vLLM, also using NIXL by default.
  • DeepSeek runs V3/R1 this way in production, with a different expert-parallel layout for each phase (details in the parallelism section below).
When it does not pay

Disaggregation adds a network hop, a second scheduler decision, and a ratio of prefill to decode GPUs that must track your traffic mix. On a handful of GPUs, or with short prompts, a unified engine with chunked prefill is usually as good and much simpler. Disaggregation wins at scale, with long prompts, strict SLOs on both TTFT and TPOT, and big MoE models where prefill and decode want different expert-parallel sizes. Many deployments disaggregate conditionally: short or fully cached prompts are prefilled locally on the decode worker.

FlashAttention: attention without the n×n matrix

The problem: attention moves too many bytes

Recall the attention computation for one head (Chapter 5): scores S = QKT/√dh, a row-wise softmax P = softmax(S), then the output O = PV. With n tokens, Q, K, V, O are [n, dh], but S and P are [n, n].

The textbook implementation is three kernels: a GEMM that writes S to HBM, a softmax kernel that reads S and writes P, and a GEMM that reads P and V. For Llama 3.1 8B at n = 8,192 in BF16:

  • One head's S is 8,192² × 2 B = 128 MiB. P is another 128 MiB. Across 32 heads that is 4 GiB per matrix per layer.
  • HBM traffic for writing and reading both: 4 × 4 GiB = 16 GiB per layer, about 5.1 ms at 3.35 TB/s.
  • The useful math (two GEMMs of 2·n²·dh FLOPs per head): ≈ 1.1 TFLOP per layer, about 1.1 ms at peak.

So the naive version is memory-bound by about 5×, even though attention at prefill is mostly matrix multiplication. And at 128K context, one head's S would be 32 GiB: it does not even fit. The softmax, the elementwise scale and the causal mask (Chapter 4 on why elementwise ops are memory-bound) only add more round trips.

The idea: never write S to memory

FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) computes exactly the same result in one fused kernel that never materializes S or P in HBM. It is the tiled matmul from Chapter 2 applied to attention. Each thread block loads a tile of query rows (say 128 rows) into on-chip SRAM, then streams through K and V one tile at a time. For each K/V tile it computes the small score tile in registers/SRAM, folds it into the running result, and discards it. Only O (plus one float per row, see below) is written back.

There is one obstacle. Softmax needs the whole row: each weight is esj divided by the sum over all j, and for numerical safety every exponent is shifted by the row maximum (Chapter 4). When you have only seen the first tile of keys, you know neither the max nor the sum. The trick that removes this obstacle is the online softmax (Milakov and Gimelshein, 2018).

The online softmax

Keep three running values for each query row: m, the largest score seen so far; ℓ, the sum of esj − m over the keys seen so far; and o, the sum of esj − m·vj (an unnormalized output vector of size dh). When a new tile of scores arrives:

mnew = max(m, maxj∈tile sj)
ℓnew = em − mnew·ℓ + Σj∈tile esj − mnew
onew = em − mnew·o + Σj∈tile esj − mnew·vj      and at the end   O = o / ℓ The factor em − mnew (≤ 1) rescales everything accumulated so far whenever a bigger score shows up, so all terms always share the same shift. When the max does not change, the factor is exactly 1. The final division by ℓ turns the weighted sum into the softmax-weighted average. The result is exact, not an approximation, up to floating-point rounding.

What it buys, in bytes

Per head at n = 8,192, the fused kernel reads Q, K, V and writes O: 4 × 8,192 × 128 × 2 B = 8 MiB, versus ≈ 512 MiB of S/P traffic for the naive version. (Each query tile does re-stream all of K and V, but a head's K and V are only 4 MiB here, and thread blocks working on the same head share them through the 50 MB L2 cache, so most re-reads never reach HBM.) Memory use drops from O(n²) to O(n), which is what makes 128K-token contexts possible at all. Attention becomes compute-bound at prefill, as it should be.

Backward pass: recompute instead of store

Training needs the attention probabilities P again for the gradients (Chapter 8). Storing them would bring back the O(n²) memory. FlashAttention stores only the per-row log-sum-exp L (one float per row) and recomputes each score tile in the backward pass from Q, K and L: Pij = esij − Li. That is more FLOPs but far fewer bytes, and on a GPU that trade wins. It is activation checkpointing at the granularity of a tile.

FlashAttention 2, 3 and 4

  • FlashAttention-2 (Dao, 2023): better work partitioning. It parallelizes over the sequence dimension too (so even one long sequence fills the GPU), splits work between warps to avoid shared-memory traffic, and defers the division by ℓ to the end to cut non-matmul FLOPs. About 2× faster than v1, reaching 50–73% of peak on A100.
  • FlashAttention-3 (Shah et al., 2024) targets Hopper (H100): asynchronous TMA copies and tensor cores, warp specialization (producer warps load tiles while consumer warps compute), and overlapping the softmax of one tile with the GEMMs of the next. It reached about 740 TFLOPS in FP16 (75% of peak) and close to 1.2 PFLOPS in FP8.
  • FlashAttention-4 (Zadouri et al., paper March 2026) targets Blackwell (B200/GB200), where tensor-core throughput doubled but exponential units and shared-memory bandwidth did not. It emulates part of the exp in software on FMA units, skips the rescale when the max barely changes ("conditional rescaling"), and uses Blackwell's tensor memory. It reports up to 1,613 TFLOPS in BF16 on B200 (71% utilization) and is written in CuTe-DSL, a Python DSL, rather than C++ templates.

Inference engines ship several attention backends (FlashAttention, FlashInfer, Triton, vendor kernels) and pick per GPU and per phase. The prefill kernels look like the one above. Decode is a different shape, which is the next section.

Key takeaway

FlashAttention is an IO optimization, not an approximation. Tile Q, stream K/V through SRAM, and keep a running max and running sum so the softmax can be computed in one pass. The n×n matrix exists only one small tile at a time, on chip.

Flash-Decoding: splitting the KV cache for long-context decode

The problem: one query row cannot fill a GPU

FlashAttention parallelizes over batch, heads and query tiles. At prefill there are thousands of query rows, so there are plenty of thread blocks. At decode there is one new query row per sequence (Chapter 5). With GQA, the 4 query heads that share a KV head are processed together, so a decode step for one sequence of Llama 3.1 8B has just 8 independent units of attention work per layer: one per KV head. An H100 has 132 SMs. At batch 1, the attention kernel lights up 8 of them, and each must walk the entire KV cache alone. At 128K context, that cache is 512 MiB per layer, and 8 SMs cannot pull 512 MiB from HBM anywhere near 3.35 TB/s: a single SM can sustain only a small fraction of total bandwidth.

The idea: parallelize over the keys too

Flash-Decoding (Dao, Haziza, Massa and Sizov, 2023) adds a parallel dimension: the KV sequence length. Split the cache into c chunks. Each thread block computes attention of the query against one chunk with the normal FlashAttention loop and writes a partial result: its own max mi, sum ℓi and unnormalized output oi (or equivalently a normalized output plus its log-sum-exp). A tiny second kernel merges the partials with the same rescaling rule as before:

M = maxi mi,   O = Σi emi − M oiΣi emi − M ℓi The online softmax is associative: partial results over disjoint key ranges can be combined in any order. That is the property that makes both the tile loop and the split-KV reduction exact.

The authors measured up to 8× faster end-to-end generation for very long sequences on CodeLlama-34B, with the attention operation itself up to 50× faster than FlashAttention-2; in their micro-benchmark, attention time stayed roughly constant up to 32K tokens. Today every serious decode kernel (FlashInfer, FlashAttention's num_splits, TensorRT-LLM's XQA, vLLM's split-KV backends) decides the split count at run time from batch size, head count and context length. When the batch is already large (say 64 sequences × 8 heads = 512 blocks) no split is needed.

Decode attention at long context is still memory-bound no matter how it is parallelized: each step must read every cached key and value once. The fix for that is fewer bytes (GQA/MLA, KV quantization; see Chapter 12), not better parallelism.

Kernel fusion, CUDA Graphs and torch.compile

Kernel fusion: fewer round trips

A transformer layer has a handful of big GEMMs and many small elementwise or reduction ops: RMSNorm, residual adds, RoPE, the SiLU-times-up product of SwiGLU, scaling, casting, writing K and V into the cache. Run naively, each op is its own kernel that reads its inputs from HBM and writes its output back. Those ops do almost no math per byte (Chapter 4), so their cost is pure memory traffic plus a kernel launch. Fusion merges a chain of them into one kernel that reads the inputs once, keeps intermediates in registers, and writes the final result once. FlashAttention is the most dramatic fusion; the everyday ones are "add residual + RMSNorm", "SiLU(gate) ⊙ up" and "RoPE + write KV cache". GEMM epilogue fusion goes further: apply the activation, scaling or (for quantized models, Chapter 13) dequantization inside the GEMM before its output is ever stored.

The launch problem in decode

Fusion also reduces the number of kernels, which matters most in decode. A decode step for Llama 3.1 8B runs on the order of 10–15 kernels per layer, so roughly 400 kernels for 32 layers plus embedding, LM head and sampling. In eager PyTorch each launch costs the CPU several microseconds (Python, framework dispatch, the CUDA driver; launch overhead itself is covered in Chapter 2). The GPU executes kernels in order, so if the CPU cannot enqueue the next kernel before the current one finishes, the GPU idles. Small kernels (a norm on a handful of rows takes 2–3 µs) finish faster than the CPU can launch the next one. The faster the GPU and the smaller the per-step work (small models, quantized weights, MoE experts, small batches), the worse this gets. vLLM's V1 announcement noted that an 8B model's whole decode step on an H100 can be as short as about 5 ms, which makes CPU overhead a first-order problem.

CUDA Graphs: record once, replay with one launch

A CUDA Graph records a sequence of kernel launches (with their arguments and dependencies) once, in a "capture" run, and then replays the entire sequence with a single launch call. The driver submits the pre-built work to the GPU with almost no per-kernel CPU cost, and the kernels run back to back. Think of it as the difference between an interpreter issuing one instruction at a time and a precompiled, pre-linked binary.

Graphs come with strict rules, and the rules shape how engines are built:

  • Fixed shapes and addresses. A captured graph bakes in tensor sizes and pointers. The engine therefore keeps static input buffers and copies each step's inputs into them, and it captures one graph per batch size. vLLM by default captures sizes 1, 2, 4, then every multiple of 8 up to 256, then every multiple of 16 up to a cap (512 by default, 1,024 on data-center Blackwell), and pads a batch of, say, 37 sequences up to the 40-graph.
  • No CPU synchronization or data-dependent control flow inside. Anything that needs the CPU to look at GPU results mid-step (dynamic shapes, Python branches on tensor values) must sit outside the graph.
  • Memory and startup cost. Each captured graph needs workspace, and capturing dozens of sizes takes time at startup.

Attention is the awkward part: its shapes depend on every sequence's context length, and prefill batches are ragged. vLLM's answer is piecewise graphs: capture everything between attention calls as graphs and run attention eagerly. Its current default mode, FULL_AND_PIECEWISE, uses a full graph (including attention, for backends that support it) for pure-decode batches and piecewise graphs for prefill and mixed batches. Decode-only workers in a disaggregated setup can use FULL_DECODE_ONLY and skip capturing the rest.

torch.compile

Writing a fused CUDA kernel for every op chain in every model is not scalable. torch.compile traces the model's Python code into a graph (TorchDynamo), and its Inductor backend generates fused Triton kernels for chains of elementwise and reduction ops automatically. vLLM compiles each model once at startup, splits the graph at attention ops (which is what makes piecewise CUDA Graphs possible), caches the compiled artifacts on disk, and adds its own compiler passes for serving-specific fusions such as merging an all-reduce with the following RMSNorm or fusing activation quantization into the preceding op. TensorRT-LLM and SGLang use a mix of hand-written kernels, torch.compile and graph capture to the same end.

Key takeaway

Fusion removes HBM round trips; CUDA Graphs remove per-kernel CPU launch costs; torch.compile generates the fused kernels automatically. In decode, where a whole step takes only a few milliseconds, the overhead they remove can be a large fraction of the step, especially for small models on fast GPUs. They are why engines pad batches to fixed sizes and spend time "capturing graphs" at startup.

Speculative decoding

The problem: decode wastes the tensor cores

At small batch, a decode step reads 16 GB of weights to do 16 GFLOP of math per sequence: the GPU is waiting on memory and its compute units are almost idle. Running the model on 5 tokens instead of 1 costs nearly the same time, because the weight read dominates (Chapter 11). The catch is that decode is sequential: token t+1 depends on token t, so you cannot just process the next 5 positions. Unless you guess them.

The idea: guess cheaply, verify in one pass

Speculative decoding (Leviathan, Kalman and Matias, 2022; and independently Chen et al. at DeepMind, 2023, as "speculative sampling") is branch prediction for language models. A cheap draft proposes the next k tokens. The expensive target model then runs one forward pass over the context plus all k guesses. Because of causal masking (Chapter 5), that single pass yields the target's next-token distribution at every one of the k+1 positions: after the context, after guess 1, after guesses 1–2, and so on. A clever accept/reject rule then keeps the longest correct prefix of guesses, plus one extra token from the target. On a good round, one target pass produces several tokens.

The remarkable part: the output is drawn from exactly the target model's distribution. Speculative decoding is not an approximation; it changes speed, never quality.

Why the output distribution is exactly the target's

Take one position. The draft proposes token x with probability q(x); we keep it with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)); otherwise we sample from r(x) = max(0, p(x) − q(x)) / Z, where Z normalizes it. The probability that the final token is x is:

P(x) = q(x)·min(1, p(x)/q(x)) + P(reject)·r(x) = min(q(x), p(x)) + max(0, p(x) − q(x)) = p(x) The middle step uses P(reject) = 1 − Σy min(p(y), q(y)) = Σy max(0, p(y) − q(y)) = Z, which cancels the normalizer of r.

Check with three tokens A, B, C: target p = (0.6, 0.3, 0.1), draft q = (0.3, 0.5, 0.2). Accept-and-keep probabilities: min(p, q) = (0.3, 0.3, 0.1), total 0.7. Rejection happens with probability 0.3, and the leftover max(0, p − q) = (0.3, 0, 0) puts all of it on A. Final: (0.3 + 0.3, 0.3, 0.1) = (0.6, 0.3, 0.1) = p. The total acceptance probability 0.7 is called the acceptance rate α; it equals 1 minus the total-variation distance between p and q. With greedy decoding (temperature 0, Chapter 14) the rule reduces to "accept while the draft's token equals the target's argmax".

How many tokens per target pass?

If each guess is accepted independently with probability α, a round yields the accepted prefix plus one token from the target (either the correction or the bonus). The expected number of tokens per target pass is a geometric sum:

E[tokens per pass] = 1 + α + α2 + … + αk = 1 − αk+11 − α α = acceptance rate per guess, k = number of drafted tokens. Example: α = 0.8, k = 4 → (1 − 0.85)/0.2 = (1 − 0.328)/0.2 = 3.36 tokens per pass. With α = 0.6 it is 2.31. As k → ∞ it approaches 1/(1 − α): 5 for α = 0.8, so long drafts have sharply diminishing returns.

The draft is not free. Let c be the cost of one draft step relative to one target step (for a 1B draft and a 70B target, both memory-bound, c is roughly the ratio of bytes read, ≈ 0.02–0.05). A round costs k·c + 1 target-step equivalents if verifying k+1 tokens costs the same as decoding 1 (true while memory-bound). The speedup in wall-clock time is:

speedup = 1 − αk+1(1 − α)(1 + k·c) This is the walltime-improvement expression from Leviathan et al. Example: α = 0.8, k = 4, c = 0.05 → 3.36 / 1.2 = 2.80×. With α = 0.6 the same setup gives 1.92×. DeepSeek-V3 drafts just k = 1 token with its multi-token-prediction module and reports an 85–90% acceptance rate, so E = 1.85–1.9, matching its reported 1.8× tokens per second.
Try it: speculative decoding speedup
Model: tokens per round = (1 − αk+1)/(1 − α); cost per round = 1 + k·c + k·β target-step units. β = 0 means verifying k+1 tokens costs the same as decoding 1 (memory-bound, small batch); β = 1 means each extra token costs a full step (compute-bound, large batch). Assumes independent acceptances; real acceptance falls with position in the draft.

Where the drafts come from

  • A separate draft model from the same family (same tokenizer), e.g. a 1B model for a 70B target. Simple and general, but it needs its own weights, its own KV cache and its own forward passes.
  • n-gram / prompt lookup: no model at all. Find the most recent occurrence of the last few tokens earlier in the context and propose whatever followed it. Free (c ≈ 0) and very effective when the output copies from the input: code edits, RAG answers quoting sources, summarization.
  • Medusa (Cai et al., 2024): extra lightweight heads on the target's last hidden state, each predicting a token further ahead. Candidates form a tree (several options per position), and the target verifies the whole tree in one pass using a tree-shaped attention mask. Reported over 2.2× (Medusa-1, frozen backbone) and 2.3–3.6× (Medusa-2).
  • EAGLE (Li et al., 2024) trains a one-layer draft that runs autoregressively on the target's own hidden features (plus the next token's embedding) instead of on tokens, which makes drafting much more accurate. EAGLE-2 adapts the draft tree's shape to the draft's confidence. EAGLE-3 (2025) predicts tokens directly from a fusion of low, middle and high-layer features and trains with "training-time test" (the draft sees its own earlier guesses during training); it reports up to 6.5× speedup and a 1.38× throughput gain at batch 64 in SGLang. EAGLE-style drafts are supported by vLLM, SGLang and TensorRT-LLM.
  • MTP heads: models pretrained with a multi-token-prediction objective (DeepSeek-V3) ship the draft module inside the checkpoint.

When it helps and when it hurts

Speculative decoding converts spare compute into lower latency. That is only a good trade when compute is spare:

  • Helps: small batches, large models, latency-sensitive traffic, predictable text (code, structured output, quoting), low temperature.
  • Hurts or vanishes: large batches. At batch 256 a decode step is already near the compute roof, so the k extra tokens per sequence cost real FLOPs, and every rejected token is wasted work that could have been another user's token (β → 1 in the widget). It also costs memory (draft weights, extra KV slots), complicates scheduling (variable tokens per step, KV rollback) and helps less at high temperature, where α drops. Engines therefore tune k per workload and may switch speculation off above a batch-size threshold.
Key takeaway

Guess k tokens cheaply, check them all in one target pass, keep the agreeing prefix plus one. The accept rule min(1, p/q) with residual resampling makes the output distribution identical to the target's. Expected tokens per pass = (1 − αk+1)/(1 − α); real speedups of 2–3× are common at low batch and shrink toward 1× as the batch grows.

Parallelism for serving

When one GPU is not enough, there are four ways to spread a model, and serving uses them differently from training (Chapter 8). Chapter 11 covered the mechanics of tensor and pipeline parallelism; here the question is which to pick for a serving goal.

  • Data parallelism (replicas). Independent copies of the model, each with its own scheduler. Throughput scales linearly, there is no communication, and a failure takes down only one replica. This is the default way to scale once a single copy fits.
  • Tensor parallelism (TP). Split every layer's weight matrices across t GPUs; two all-reduces per layer combine the partial results. Each GPU reads 1/t of the weights per step, so TP reduces per-token latency, not just memory. The all-reduces happen ~160 times per step for an 80-layer model, so TP lives inside one NVLink domain (8 GPUs in an HGX server, 72 in a GB200 NVL72 rack).
  • Pipeline parallelism (PP). Put different layers on different machines. Communication is small (one activation hand-off per stage), so it crosses nodes cheaply, but a single sequence still visits the stages one after another, so it adds latency instead of removing it. With many sequences in flight, all stages stay busy.
  • Expert parallelism (EP). For MoE models (Chapter 7), place different experts on different GPUs and route tokens to them with an all-to-all dispatch before each MoE layer and a combine after it.

Big MoE models: data-parallel attention plus wide EP

For a model like DeepSeek-V3 (671B total, 37B active, 256 routed experts, top-8, MLA attention), the best serving layout is neither pure TP nor pure EP. Two observations drive it.

Attention wants data parallelism. MLA compresses each token's keys and values into one shared latent vector (Chapter 5), so there are no separate KV heads to split. Under TP, every GPU would need a full copy of every sequence's latent KV cache: t copies of the same bytes. Instead, engines run attention data-parallel ("DP-attention", introduced in SGLang v0.4): each GPU runs attention only for its own subset of requests and holds only their KV cache.

Experts want huge batches. At decode, a step with B sequences sends about B × 8 / 256 = B/32 tokens to each routed expert. For an expert's GEMM to be efficient it needs on the order of a hundred or more tokens, so B must be in the thousands. No single GPU can hold the KV cache for thousands of sequences, but a few hundred GPUs together can, if each GPU holds only a few experts (leaving the rest of its memory for KV cache). That is wide EP (the tokens-per-expert argument comes from Chapter 7).

The DeepSeek layout, and keeping experts balanced

DeepSeek's V3 technical report describes a minimum prefill unit of 4 nodes (32 GPUs: attention with TP4 plus sequence parallelism and DP8, MoE with EP32) and a minimum decode unit of 40 nodes (320 GPUs, EP320). Their February 2025 production overview uses prefill units of 4 nodes with EP32 (each GPU holds 9 routed experts and 1 shared expert) and decode units of 18 nodes with EP144 (2 routed experts and 1 shared expert per GPU). Two problems come with that scale, and both have standard answers now:

  • Hot experts. Real traffic does not spread evenly over 256 experts. The GPU holding a popular expert gets more tokens and the whole step waits for it. The fix is redundant experts: duplicate the hottest experts on extra GPUs (DeepSeek uses 32 redundant experts) and periodically (every ~10 minutes) recompute the placement from observed load. DeepSeek open-sourced this as EPLB, and SGLang and vLLM implement it. On top, load balancers keep the attention side even: equal KV usage and request counts per DP rank.
  • Communication time. Two all-to-alls per MoE layer cross the network. Two-batch overlap splits each step's batch into two micro-batches and alternates them, so one micro-batch's all-to-all runs while the other computes. DeepSeek's decode path uses a deeper 5-stage pipeline of the same idea.

The payoff is real: the SGLang team reproduced this design on 96 H100s (12 nodes) and reported 52.3k input tokens/s and 22.3k output tokens/s per node for 2,000-token prompts, which they estimated at about $0.20 per million output tokens.

Key takeaway

Replicate when the model fits; use TP inside a node to cut per-token latency; use PP to cross nodes; for big MoE models, run attention data-parallel and spread experts wide so each expert sees a big batch, then fight load imbalance with redundant experts and hide all-to-all traffic with micro-batch overlap.

The cluster: routing, autoscaling and cold starts

Prefix-aware routing

Prefix caching (Chapter 12) lets a replica skip prefill for any prompt prefix it has already computed: a shared system prompt, tool definitions, or the earlier turns of a conversation. But each replica's cache is local. With 50 replicas behind a round-robin load balancer, the second turn of a chat lands on a random replica and the hit rate collapses to about 1/50. The router must therefore send requests with the same prefix to the same place.

  • Hash-based affinity: hash a session ID or the first N tokens and use consistent hashing. Cheap, but blind to load.
  • Approximate cache view: SGLang's cache-aware router keeps an approximate radix tree of what each worker has cached and picks the best match, reporting up to 1.9× throughput and 3.8× higher hit rate versus round-robin.
  • Exact cache view: engines publish KV-block events (allocated, evicted) and the router indexes them (llm-d's "precise prefix-cache" routing, Dynamo's KV-aware router, which scores each worker by overlap and load).

Pure affinity creates hot spots (one popular system prompt can overload one replica), so every real router blends cache overlap with load signals: queued tokens, running sequences, KV-cache utilization. Note the unit: tokens in flight, not requests, because one request can be 100,000 times bigger than another.

Autoscaling

Traffic for a public model swings by several times between day and night. DeepSeek's published statistics show a peak of 278 nodes and an average of 226.75 nodes over one day, with inference capacity handed to research and training at night. Good scaling signals are the ones that predict SLO violations: length of the waiting queue, KV-cache utilization (close to 100% means preemptions are coming), and measured TTFT percentiles. GPU utilization is a poor signal because a decode-heavy GPU looks "100% busy" while it is mostly waiting on memory. With disaggregation, prefill and decode pools scale separately (Dynamo's planner does this from TTFT and ITL targets).

Cold starts

A new replica cannot serve until its weights are in HBM and its kernels are ready: fetch, disk read, PCIe copy, then compilation and CUDA Graph capture (Chapter 11 times each hop). For a 70B model that is anywhere from well under a minute (everything cached on local disk) to many minutes, and it is the autoscaler's reaction time. Providers hide it with warm spare capacity, local weight caches, streaming loaders that copy safetensors shards straight into GPU memory, persistent compile caches, and sleep/wake modes that offload a model's weights to CPU RAM instead of tearing the process down.

The economics of a token

From GPU-hours to dollars per million tokens

A serving provider pays for GPU time, whether or not the GPUs do useful work. Everything else (power, networking, staff, margin, paying back training) sits on top, but the core unit cost is simple:

$ per million tokens = GPU $/hour × number of GPUstokens per second × 3,600 × utilization × 106 "Tokens per second" is the aggregate throughput of the replica at its operating point; "utilization" is the fraction of paid time the replica is actually serving at that rate (traffic is bursty, so it is always below 1).

Work it through for Llama 3.1 8B (BF16) on one H100 at an assumed $3.00 per hour, roughly the late-2026 market rate (rental indexes and price trackers show H100s mostly between $2 and $4 per GPU-hour).

Why output tokens are priced several times input

Chapter 11 gives the compute reason: an input token rides in a compute-bound prefill pass with thousands of others, while an output token takes one slot in a memory-bound decode step and holds its KV cache in HBM for the whole answer, which caps the batch. Cached input, batch and priority: what each discount buys

  • Cached input (90–97.5% off). A prefix-cache hit (Chapter 12) skips the prefill compute for those tokens entirely; the KV blocks are read instead of recomputed. The catch is storage: cached KV occupies HBM (or CPU RAM and SSD), which is the scarce resource that limits batch size. So caches expire after minutes, longer retention costs more, and some providers charge a premium (1.25–2.5× input) to write the cache. Reads cost 0.025–0.1× of the input price, depending on model; Chapter 12 has the per-provider rules.
  • Batch APIs (50% off). OpenAI, Anthropic and Google all charge half price for asynchronous jobs with a completion window of up to 24 hours. With no latency SLO, the provider can run these at the largest batch sizes and in traffic troughs, filling GPUs that are paid for anyway. DeepSeek's off-peak discount is the same idea expressed as a time of day.
  • Priority / fast tiers (2–6× more). Faster tokens per user means smaller batches (or more GPUs per replica, or more aggressive speculation), so fewer tokens per GPU-second. OpenAI's "Fast" tier (renamed from Priority processing in July 2026) is 2× standard prices and its "Ultrafast" tier 6×; Anthropic's fast mode for Claude Opus 5.5 is $8/$40, 2× its standard price. The calculator below shows why: moving left-to-right on the latency axis is a steep climb in cost.
  • Long-context surcharges. A 200K-token request holds as much KV cache as a hundred 2K-token requests and its attention costs grow with length. Gemini 3.1 Pro charges $4/$18 above 200K input tokens instead of $2/$12, and Claude Haiku 5.5 charges more for prompts above 100K tokens.

Rate limits

Rate limits are admission control at the cluster level, the same job the scheduler does inside one replica. Capacity is a provisioned number of GPUs; the limits keep one customer's burst from breaking everyone else's SLOs. OpenAI measures requests per minute (RPM), tokens per minute (TPM) and per-day variants, with limits rising with your usage tier; when admitting a request it counts the larger of your max_tokens and the estimated length, so an inflated max_tokens eats quota. Anthropic limits requests per minute and splits tokens into input tokens per minute (ITPM) and output tokens per minute (OTPM), because output is the scarcer resource; for most Claude models, cache-read tokens do not count toward ITPM, which makes caching raise your effective limit. Both use a token-bucket algorithm (capacity refills continuously rather than resetting each minute) and return HTTP 429 with a retry-after hint; batch jobs have separate queue limits. Clients should back off exponentially and spread bursts.

The throughput–latency Pareto frontier

Every serving deployment picks a point on a curve. Small batches give each user fast tokens but waste the weight read; large batches are cheap per token but slow per user, and eventually run out of KV memory. There is no point that is both fastest and cheapest, only a frontier of best trade-offs. For our 8B example with 2,048-token contexts: batch 1 gives ≈ 164 tokens/s to its single user at ≈ $5.08 per million tokens; batch 64 gives ≈ 81 tokens/s each at ≈ $0.16; batch 200 (near the memory limit) gives ≈ 38 tokens/s each at ≈ $0.11. Halving the per-user speed made tokens over 30× cheaper. The calculator lets you move along this curve. Try a B200 with 512-token contexts and batch 512: decode becomes nearly compute-bound and the output/input cost gap almost closes. In real traffic, long contexts (big KV reads, small batches) and latency targets keep the gap wide.

A real data point: DeepSeek's published numbers

In February 2025 DeepSeek published one day of production statistics for V3/R1 (all on H800 GPUs, FP8 matmuls, prefill/decode disaggregated):

  • Average 226.75 nodes × 8 GPUs; at an assumed $2 per GPU-hour that is $87,072 per day.
  • 608B input tokens, of which 342B (56.3%) hit the on-disk KV cache; 168B output tokens. Average output speed per user was only 20–22 tokens/s, with an average KV length of 4,989 tokens per output token.
  • Per node: ~73.7k input tokens/s during prefill (including cache hits) or ~14.8k output tokens/s during decode.

Plug the node rates into the formula: a node costs 8 × $2 = $16/hour, so input costs $16 ÷ (73,700 × 3,600) × 106 ≈ $0.060 per million and output $16 ÷ (14,800 × 3,600) × 106 ≈ $0.30 per million. That is a 5× ratio from real hardware, right in line with list prices. DeepSeek computed that if every token were billed at R1 prices the day would bring $562,027 of revenue, a 545% "cost profit margin", while noting actual revenue is much lower (web and app use is free, V3 is cheaper, night discounts apply). Note also the slow 20–22 tokens/s per user: it is exactly the low-cost end of the Pareto frontier.

Key takeaway

$ per token = $ per GPU-second ÷ tokens per GPU-second. Input tokens are cheap because prefill is compute-bound and massively parallel; output tokens cost ~5× more because decode is memory-bound and serial. Caching skips prefill compute, batch tiers fill idle capacity, and fast tiers buy smaller batches. Per-user speed and price sit on one Pareto curve.
